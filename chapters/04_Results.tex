\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

The experiments described in \cref{sec:programme} were completed on both cooling
loops. This chapter presents the results of the baseline thermal comparison
(Experiment~1) and their comparison with the thermal network, followed by the
results of the inductor experiment (Experiment~2) and of the conjugate heat
transfer simulation. Unless stated otherwise, all temperatures are steady-state
thermocouple readings, with an accuracy of
$\pm\SI{2.2}{\celsius}$~\cite{loh_project_2025}. The interpretation of these
results is left to \cref{sec:discussion}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experiment 1: Thermal Comparison}
\label{sec:results-exp1}

Six steady-state tests were completed: two with Galinstan and four with water.

\subsection{Measured Temperatures}
\label{sec:results-measured}

\Cref{fig:water_transient} presents the left and right resistor temperatures and
the cold-plate inlet and outlet temperatures over each water test, and
\cref{fig:galinstan_transient} presents the same temperatures over the Galinstan
tests. In each water test, the resistor temperature rose quickly over the first
30 minutes and then levelled off as the whole loop warmed, with the inlet
temperature rising from room temperature to about
\SIrange{32}{34}{\celsius}. Steady state was reached after about 60 to 90
minutes, after which the heat load was switched off and the loop cooled back
towards ambient.

The full-flow Galinstan test (\cref{fig:galinstan_transient}a) followed the same
pattern, reaching steady state after about 60 minutes with the resistors at
about \SI{59}{\celsius} and the inlet at \SI{29.8}{\celsius}. Unlike in the
water tests, the coolant left the plate about \SI{18}{\celsius} warmer than it
entered, and the right-hand resistor ran about \SI{1.5}{\celsius} warmer than the
left. The low-flow test at \SI{40}{\watt} (\cref{fig:galinstan_transient}b)
warmed more slowly and approached steady state after about 75 minutes, with the
resistors at about \SI{51}{\celsius}, the inlet at \SI{26.5}{\celsius} and the
coolant again rising by about \SI{17}{\celsius} across the plate. The right-hand
resistor again ran slightly warmer than the left, by about \SI{1}{\celsius}.

\Cref{tab:measured-performance} summarises the steady-state values. For water,
the temperature rise of the resistor above the cold-plate inlet,
$\Tres - \Tin$, was almost constant, between \SI{16.1}{\celsius} and
\SI{16.6}{\celsius}, across flow rates from \SI{58.3}{\milli\litre\per\second}
to \SI{8.3}{\milli\litre\per\second}. The temperature rise of the water itself,
$\Tout - \Tin$, was below \SI{1}{\celsius} in every test. This is within the
thermocouple accuracy, which is why two of the tests show a small negative
value. For Galinstan, the resistor rose \SI{29.5}{\celsius} above the inlet at
full flow (\SI{72}{\watt}) and \SI{24.6}{\celsius} at low flow (\SI{40}{\watt}),
and the coolant itself rose by \SI{17.8}{\celsius} and \SI{17.1}{\celsius}
across the plate.

Dividing the resistor temperature rise by the heat reaching the cold plate,
$\Qplate$, gives the total thermal resistance from the resistor to the coolant
inlet, $(\Tres - \Tin)/\Qplate = \Rpack + \Rcp$. With Galinstan, the total
resistance was \SI{0.488}{\celsius\per\watt} at full flow and
\SI{0.799}{\celsius\per\watt} at low flow, compared with
\SIrange{0.257}{0.267}{\celsius\per\watt} for water. At the flow rates achieved,
Galinstan therefore cooled the resistors less effectively than water, with a
total resistance 1.8 to 3.1 times higher.

\subsection{Comparison with the Thermal Network}
\label{sec:results-tn}

\Cref{tab:tn_results} compares the measured resistor and outlet temperatures
with the predictions of the thermal network of \cref{eq:network}, using
$\Rpack = \SI{0.242}{\celsius\per\watt}$ in every test. The two turbulent water
tests were used to calibrate $\Rpack$. The Galinstan flow, measured with the EMF
sensor, agreed with the energy balance to within \SI{4}{\percent}. For
Galinstan, the model predicts the resistor temperature exactly at low flow and
\SI{2.5}{\celsius} high at full flow, close to the $\pm\SI{2.2}{\celsius}$
thermocouple accuracy. The two laminar water tests are over-predicted by
\SI{3.0}{\celsius} and \SI{3.6}{\celsius}.

\Cref{fig:galinstan_rcp,fig:water_rcp} present the measured cold-plate
resistance, inferred with \cref{eq:rcp-inferred}, against flow rate, together
with the $\varepsilon$--NTU model. Because $\Rpack$ is common to every
test, the difference between the coolants lies in the cold plate. Galinstan's
measured $\Rcp$ (\SIrange{0.25}{0.56}{\celsius\per\watt}) was between about 10
and 37 times that of water (\SIrange{0.015}{0.025}{\celsius\per\watt}). The
low-flow Galinstan point lies on the model curve, while the full-flow point lies
about \SI{0.05}{\celsius\per\watt} below it. For water, the two turbulent points
follow the datasheet trend. The two low-flow points, however, lie well below the
laminar $\varepsilon$--NTU prediction of \SIrange{0.074}{0.088}{\celsius\per\watt},
close to an extrapolation of the turbulent datasheet trend. In other words, the
measured resistor temperature barely changed as the water flow fell from
\SI{58.3}{\milli\litre\per\second} to \SI{8.3}{\milli\litre\per\second}.

These results differ from those of Hodes \emph{et al.}, whose model of
geometrically optimised heat sinks for direct die cooling found that Galinstan
reduced the total thermal resistance by about \SI{40}{\percent} relative to
water at the same pressure drop, and by about \SI{20}{\percent} at the same
pumping power~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Their total resistance was
\SI{0.050}{\celsius\per\watt} with Galinstan and \SI{0.074}{\celsius\per\watt}
with water. Importantly, their optimised Galinstan heat sink was far less
caloric-limited than the cold plate tested here. Driven at a pressure drop of
\SI{214}{\kilo\pascal} into turbulent flow, its caloric resistance made up about
\SI{55}{\percent} of the total, whereas in this study, at laminar flows in a cold
plate designed for water, the caloric resistance makes up almost all of $\Rcp$
(\cref{sec:disc-performance}), and the Galinstan total resistance was 1.8 to 3.1
times that of water. The comparison is, however, limited by the very different
scales of the two systems. Hodes \emph{et al.}\ modelled a
\SI{1}{\centi\metre} by \SI{1}{\centi\metre} heat sink with channels
\SI{302}{\micro\metre} tall for direct cooling of a microprocessor
die~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}, whereas the cold plate tested here has a heated
area of about \SI{150}{\centi\metre\squared} and \SI{8}{\milli\metre} tubes
(\cref{tab:coolant-properties}).

\subsection{Pumping Power}
\label{sec:results-pump}

\Cref{tab:pumps} compares the pumps of the two loops. The MHD pump drew
\SI{0.85}{\watt} at the full-flow current of \SI{18}{\ampere}, measured across
the pump terminals, and \SI{0.2}{\watt} at \SI{9}{\ampere}. The water pump drew
\SI{11.5}{\watt} at full flow and \SI{2.54}{\watt} at
\SI{29.2}{\milli\litre\per\second}. The MHD pump was also more than 50 times
smaller than the water pump, but its maximum mechanical efficiency was only
\SI{0.56}{\percent}, compared with \SI{28.7}{\percent} for the centrifugal
water pump; the pump volumes and efficiencies are detailed in Ekbote's
report~\cite{ekbote_project_2026}. The coefficient of performance (COP), the
ratio of the heat load to the pump power, reached 200 for the Galinstan loop at
low flow (\SI{40}{\watt} at \SI{0.2}{\watt}), against 47.6 for the water loop,
measured in an additional test at \SI{110}{\watt} using the same methodology as
the other steady-state tests.

\Cref{tab:fan-comparison} compares these results with the liquid metal cooling
systems of Fan \emph{et al.}, which used permanent-magnet MHD pumps to cool SiC
converters~\cite{fan_liquid_2021,fan_high_2023,fan_ultra-efficient_2026}. Their
pumps removed \SIrange{60}{280}{\watt} at gallium-alloy flows of up to about
\SI{10}{\milli\litre\per\second} while drawing less than \SI{0.1}{\watt},
whereas the pump of this study drew \SI{0.85}{\watt} to remove \SI{72}{\watt}
at \SI{1.4}{\milli\litre\per\second}. Their pumps therefore moved several times
more coolant for less than an eighth of the power. Their water comparisons show
the same trend as here: the water pumps drew \SIrange{4.2}{8}{\watt} at
\SIrange{62}{80}{\milli\litre\per\second}, against \SI{11.5}{\watt} at
\SI{58.3}{\milli\litre\per\second} in this study. In Fan \emph{et al.}'s
systems, however, the liquid metal also matched the cooling of water, with
device junction temperatures of \SI{54}{\celsius} against \SI{59}{\celsius} and
\SI{53.0}{\celsius} against \SI{53.2}{\celsius}~\cite{fan_liquid_2021}, whereas
here the Galinstan total resistance was 1.8 to 3.1 times that of water.

\section{Experiment 2: Inductor Cooling}
\label{sec:results-exp2}

\subsection{Thermal Performance}
\label{sec:results-inductor-thermal}

\Cref{fig:inductor-cam} presents thermal camera images of the inductor on the
Galinstan cold plate, driven at \SI{12}{\ampere} and \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz} with
the Galinstan at full flow. The images were taken at steady state, with the
camera emissivity set to 0.95. The maximum surface temperatures were
\SI{80.8}{\celsius} and \SI{83.0}{\celsius} in the two views. The upper core and
the top of the winding are the hottest regions, and the temperature falls
towards the cold plate, which appears much cooler than the inductor. This is the
pattern expected from the asymmetric cooling described in
\cref{sec:inductor-thermal}: the lower core is cooled by the plate, while the
upper core relies on convection to the surrounding air and on conduction across
the gap to the lower core. The temperature difference between the upper and
lower cores in \cref{fig:inductor-cam-a} therefore shows the cooling effect of
the Galinstan cold plate over natural convection. The flow rate did not change
measurably when the inductor was energised.

\subsection{Eddy-Current Loss}
\label{sec:results-eddy}

The absolute AC resistance of the winding varied between measurement sessions,
so each configuration is compared with the nominal resistance of the inductor
alone, measured in the same session. \Cref{fig:rac_configurations} presents the
increase in $R_{\mathrm{ac}}$ above this nominal value for each configuration.
Placing the inductor on the empty cold plate increased $R_{\mathrm{ac}}$ by
\SI{11.7}{\milli\ohm}. Filling the plate with Galinstan raised the increase by
only a further \SI{1.0}{\milli\ohm}, to \SI{12.7}{\milli\ohm}. With the
Galinstan vial beside the inductor, the increase rose to \SI{22.0}{\milli\ohm},
\SI{10.3}{\milli\ohm} more than with the empty plate.

In relative terms, the empty cold plate increased $R_{\mathrm{ac}}$ by about
\SI{15}{\percent} of its nominal value, and the Galinstan within it added about
\SI{1}{\percent} more. Gao \emph{et al.} measured a larger effect for a planar
inductor mounted on a liquid-cooled aluminium cold plate: its AC resistance rose
from \SI{35.4}{\milli\ohm} when passively cooled to \SI{47.5}{\milli\ohm} on the
cold plate, an increase of \SI{34}{\percent} at about \SI{250}{\kilo\hertz},
against \SIrange{15}{19}{\percent} predicted by their simulations~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.
They note that this extra loss arises in the aluminium parts, which conduct
heat directly to the coolant, so it does not compromise the thermal design, and
that structures near the air gap must be kept clear of its fringing field.

\section{Conjugate Heat Transfer Simulation}
\label{sec:results-cht}

The CHT model was run for the two Galinstan tests and the full-flow water test,
on the \num{427e3}-cell mesh. Its mesh independence, iterative convergence and
detailed comparison with the measured probe temperatures are given in
\cref{app:cht_validation}. For both Galinstan tests, the simulated probe
temperatures agree with the measurements within the $\pm\SI{2.2}{\celsius}$
thermocouple accuracy. For the full-flow water test, the simulated inlet and
outlet temperatures also agree, but the resistor temperatures are under-predicted
by \SIrange{2.3}{2.4}{\celsius}, just outside the thermocouple accuracy.

\Cref{fig:galinstan_rcp_cfd} compares the Galinstan cold-plate resistance from
the simulations with the $\varepsilon$--NTU model and the measured values, all
obtained with \cref{eq:rcp-inferred} (\cref{sec:rcp-calc}). At the two tested
flows, the simulated resistance lies between the model and the measurements.
At \SI{2.8}{\milli\litre\per\second} and \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second},
beyond the flows the rig could reach, the simulated values lie a steady
\SIrange{0.035}{0.036}{\celsius\per\watt} below the model, but follow its
dependence on flow. Over the whole range, the simulation therefore confirms that
the Galinstan cold plate is caloric-limited, with $\Rcp$ inversely proportional
to the flow rate.
