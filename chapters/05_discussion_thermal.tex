\chapter{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results of \cref{ch:results} against the two
research questions. The performance of the two coolants is compared first,
followed by the accuracy of the thermal models and their use to extend the
results beyond the flows the apparatus could reach. The effect of reducing the
heated area is then used to identify where in an IPT system Galinstan cooling is
most useful. The chapter closes with the limitations of the study and
recommendations for further work.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Comparison of Galinstan and Water Cooling}
\label{sec:disc-performance}

In the experiments, water outperformed Galinstan across the whole operating
range. The total resistance from the resistor to the coolant inlet was
\SIrange{0.488}{0.799}{\celsius\per\watt} with Galinstan, against
\SIrange{0.257}{0.267}{\celsius\per\watt} with water, so the Galinstan loop's
resistance was 1.8 to 3.1 times higher (\cref{tab:measured-performance}). The
Galinstan tests, however, ran at
\SIrange{0.77}{1.45}{\milli\litre\per\second}, one to two orders of magnitude
below the water tests (\SIrange{8.3}{58.3}{\milli\litre\per\second}). The
comparison therefore reflects the flow the MHD pump could deliver rather than a
general disadvantage of Galinstan as a coolant.

The $\varepsilon$--NTU model explains why (\cref{tab:ntu-regimes}). Galinstan
had an NTU of 35 to 65 in the tests, so its cold plate is caloric-limited: the
coolant leaves the plate at almost the plate temperature, and heat removal is
limited by the heat-capacity rate of the flow, $\mdot\,\cp$. This is what was
seen in practice. The Galinstan rose by \SIrange{17}{18}{\celsius} across the
plate (\cref{fig:galinstan_transient}), and the large temperature gradient this
creates across the plate significantly reduced its performance. Water, by
contrast, had an NTU of 0.3 to 0.4 at every tested flow, so its cold plate is
convection-limited, and the water rose by less than \SI{1}{\celsius}. Galinstan's
high thermal conductivity gives it a wall heat transfer coefficient about 7.5
times that of laminar water (\cref{app:convection}), but this advantage is not
used in the tested cold plate, as the convective term makes up less than
\SI{3}{\percent} of its $\Rcp$. These results support H1: Galinstan lowers the
convective resistance, but its low volumetric heat capacity and low flow give a
caloric resistance large enough to make the total resistance of the Galinstan
loop higher than that of the water loop.

The two regimes also respond very differently to flow. Increasing the water flow
had only a minimal effect: the total resistance fell from 0.267 to
\SI{0.257}{\celsius\per\watt} over a seven-fold increase in flow. Small changes
in the Galinstan flow, in contrast, had a significant impact, with the total
resistance falling from 0.799 to \SI{0.488}{\celsius\per\watt} as the flow
roughly doubled from 0.77 to \SI{1.45}{\milli\litre\per\second}. Because the
pressure of an MHD pump scales directly with its current
(\cref{sec:em-pumping}), the Galinstan loop gives direct control of the component
temperature through the pump current. This is an advantage where the
temperature swing, rather than the peak temperature, limits component lifetime
(\cref{sec:reliability}).

RQ1 also asks how the pumping power of the two loops compares. At full flow, the
MHD pump drew \SI{0.85}{\watt}, giving a maximum COP of 200 against 47.6 for water
(\cref{tab:pumps}). The low power draw does not, however, reflect an efficient
pump: its maximum mechanical efficiency of \SI{0.56}{\percent} is an order of
magnitude below the \SIrange{6}{10}{\percent} reported for DC conduction
pumps~\cite{an_liquid_2024,hodes_potential_2014}. The pump was also already at
the \SI{18}{\ampere} limit of its supply at full flow, so higher flows would
require a redesigned pump, or a more powerful supply. Fan \emph{et al.}\ show what is achievable: by
reducing the pressure drop of their loops to about one sixth, their MHD pumps
reached flows of up to about \SI{10}{\milli\litre\per\second} for less than
\SI{0.1}{\watt}~\cite{fan_liquid_2021,fan_high_2023} (\cref{tab:fan-comparison}).

\section{Accuracy and Extension of the Models}
\label{sec:disc-models}

The analytical thermal network is only accurate close to the conditions it was
calibrated against. It reproduced the turbulent water tests used to calibrate
$\Rpack$, and predicted the Galinstan resistor temperatures within
\SI{2.5}{\celsius}, but over-predicted the two low-flow water tests by
\SIrange{3.0}{3.6}{\celsius} (\cref{tab:tn_results}). Close placement of the pump 
and flow restrictor likely caused turbulence that remained in the cold plate since
laminar flow could not fully develop during low-flow testing. 
Additionally, the assumption of an isothermal plate does not hold with an
\SI{18}{\celsius} coolant temperature rise seen in the Galinstan plates.

A conjugate heat transfer model was therefore created in SimScale to capture the
performance more fully, by resolving the temperature field in the plate and
coolant (\cref{sec:cht}). It was more consistent across the tests than the
analytical model, reproducing all four probe temperatures within
\SI{1.4}{\celsius} for both Galinstan tests and under-predicting the water
resistor temperatures by only about \SI{2.3}{\celsius}
(\cref{app:cht_validation}). There was also a lack of good data for validation, so only two tests were used to fit the model.
Despite this, the CHT model was used to extend the Galinstan flow to
\SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}, the upper bound of laminar flow in the tube
($\mathrm{Re} \approx 2110$). Turbulent Galinstan flow was not assessed, as no
data were available to validate the accuracy of the turbulence model for a
liquid metal.

The simulations show a significant improvement at \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}
(\cref{fig:galinstan_rcp_cfd}). The simulated $\Rcp$ falls from
\SI{0.28}{\celsius\per\watt} at \SI{1.4}{\milli\litre\per\second} to
\SI{0.050}{\celsius\per\watt}, nearly six times lower, and the coolant
temperature rise falls from about \SI{18}{\celsius} to about \SI{5}{\celsius}.
With the same package resistance, the total resistance from the resistor to the
inlet would be about \SI{0.29}{\celsius\per\watt}, only 10 to \SI{14}{\percent}
above the \SIrange{0.257}{0.267}{\celsius\per\watt} measured in the resistor
tests with water (\cref{tab:measured-performance}). At the \SI{60}{\watt}
reaching the plate, the resistors would run about \SI{17.5}{\celsius} above the
inlet, against \SIrange{16.1}{16.6}{\celsius} with water, at a lower flow than
any of the water tests. The flow required is, however, about 3.6 times the full
flow of the existing pump.

\section{Effect of Heated Area}
\label{sec:disc-area}

The analytical model can be extended somewhat to show how the coolants compare when the heated area
is reduced (\cref{fig:area-scaling}). The heated area is scaled by a factor $f$
while the flow rate and heat transfer coefficient are held at their values for
the tested cold plate, so that $\NTU = f\,\NTU_0$ and
$\Rcp = 1/[\mdot\,\cp\,(1 - e^{-f\,\NTU_0})]$ (\cref{app:area-scaling}). Water at
\SI{30}{\milli\litre\per\second} starts at $\NTU_0 = 0.35$, so its resistance
rises almost in proportion to $1/f$. Galinstan starts at $\NTU_0 = 34$ at
\SI{1.5}{\milli\litre\per\second} and 11 at \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}, so
its resistance remains close to the caloric limit until $f\,\NTU_0$ falls to
about 3. Galinstan is therefore shown to outperform water once the heated area is reduced
below about \SI{7}{\percent} of the tested plate, about \SI{11}{\centi\metre\squared},
at \SI{1.5}{\milli\litre\per\second}, and below about \SI{26}{\percent}, about
\SI{40}{\centi\metre\squared}, at \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/area_scaling}
  \caption{Cold-plate resistance against heated area.}
  \label{fig:area-scaling}
  \floatnote{$\varepsilon$--NTU model at fixed flow and $h$. Dotted line: tested
    plate area (\SI{153}{\centi\metre\squared}).}

A coolant that starts with a large NTU can therefore absorb a large reduction in
area, and hence a large increase in heat flux, before its performance suffers.
This agrees with Hodes \emph{et al.}, whose advantage for Galinstan depended on
small heat sinks optimised to exploit its high
conductivity~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Because $h$ is held fixed, the plate is
assumed isothermal and pressure drop is not considered, the result is indicative
only, but it shows the type of application in which Galinstan becomes useful:
small, high-flux footprints at flows a redesigned pump could deliver.

\section{Applications in IPT Systems}
\label{sec:disc-suitability}

RQ2 asks whether the Galinstan loop can cool a power-dense inductor while adding
minimal loss. The inductor operated at an estimated loss of \SI{42.76}{\watt},
more than twice the \SI{17.88}{\watt} above which its core requires active
cooling (\cref{sec:analytical-design}), with a maximum surface temperature of
about \SI{83}{\celsius}. The upper core, which relies on natural convection, ran
markedly hotter than the lower core on the cold plate
(\cref{fig:inductor-cam-a}). This supports the first part of H2, that the loop
was demonstrated to cool the inductor beyond the performance of natural convection. Filling the cold plate with
Galinstan added only \SI{1.0}{\milli\ohm} to the winding resistance, within the
$\pm\SI{1.5}{\milli\ohm}$ repeatability of the LCR meter, against
\SI{11.7}{\milli\ohm} for the plate itself and \SI{10.3}{\milli\ohm} for
unshielded Galinstan beside the inductor (\cref{sec:results-eddy}). The metal of
the cold plate, several skin depths thick at \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz}, therefore
shields the coolant from the leakage field, which supports the second part of
H2. Unshielded Galinstan, with a skin depth of about \SI{0.9}{\milli\metre} at
this frequency, is far more exposed, although its effect fell from
\SI{9.0}{\milli\ohm} at \SI{1}{\centi\metre} to \SI{2.15}{\milli\ohm} beyond
\SI{4}{\centi\metre} (\cref{fig:rac_distance}). This suggests that a Galinstan 
coolant could be used in PECs where the loss of a cold plate is already accepted, like that 
in Gao \emph{et al.}'s inductor design~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.  The final part of H2, that the
field does not change the flow, could not be confirmed: the EMF reading
suggested that the flow fell as the inductor current rose, but the signal is in
the microvolt range and sensitive to noise, so it is possible the flow did not change.

Together with the area analysis, these results indicate where Galinstan cooling
is likely to be most useful in an IPT system (\cref{tab:disc-suitability}). It
suits small, high-flux components where water would be convection-limited, such
as the inverter and rectifier, which dissipate \SIrange{70}{80}{\watt} each in a
small area away from the coupler~\cite{kim_thermal_2022}. It is less suited to
large, distributed heat loads such as the pads, spread over about
\SI{0.5}{\metre\squared}~\cite{kim_thermal_2022}, where water is adequate and
Galinstan's low volumetric heat capacity dominates: per unit volume, it carries
only about \SI{55}{\percent} of the heat that water does
(\cref{tab:coolant-properties}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

\paragraph{Amount of data.} The main limitation is the small amount of
experimental data. Only two Galinstan and four water steady-state tests were
run, each once (\cref{app:test-log}), so the repeatability of the measurements
is unknown, and only the instrument accuracies could be propagated into the
measured resistances (\cref{app:uncertainty}). The models
were calibrated and checked against these same few points: $\Rpack$ was fitted
to two water tests. The agreement between model and measurement is therefore encouraging
but not conclusive, and more experiments, repeated and spread across a wider
range of flows and heat loads, would be required to be confident in the models,
particularly where they are extrapolated to faster flows, up to \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}, and
to smaller heated areas.

\paragraph{Flow measurement.} No accuracy specification could be found for the
water flow meter; its documentation states only that flows below
\SI{1}{\litre\per\minute} (\SI{16.7}{\milli\litre\per\second}) are out of range.
The two low-flow water tests, at 12.2 and \SI{8.3}{\milli\litre\per\second},
were therefore measured outside the meter's range, and their flows, Reynolds
numbers and inferred $\Rcp$ are uncertain, which may explain why they lie close
to the turbulent trend rather than the laminar prediction
(\cref{fig:water_rcp}). The Galinstan flow relies on an EMF in the microvolt
range, which is sensitive to noise, although it agreed with the energy balance
to within about \SI{6}{\percent} (\cref{app:uncertainty}). The water temperature rise across the plate was also
below the thermocouple accuracy, so the water energy balance could not be
checked independently.

\paragraph{Models.} $\Rpack$ was calibrated on water, where the plate is close
to isothermal, whereas Galinstan's \SIrange{17}{18}{\celsius} coolant
temperature rise does not seem to follow this assumption. The CHT model was run only for
laminar Galinstan flow, as there were no data to validate a turbulence model for
a liquid metal. The area analysis holds $h$ fixed and ignores spreading in the
plate and pressure drop, so it is indicative only.

\paragraph{Scope.} A single cold plate, designed for water, was tested, and the
coolants were compared at the flow each pump could deliver rather than at equal
pumping power. The inductor test was run at a single operating point, with a
heat load estimated from the core-loss model rather than measured, so the
thermal performance of the loop around the inductor could only be estimated
roughly: the coolant carried about half of the estimated loss
(\cref{app:inductor-data}).

\section{Recommendations for Further Work}
\label{sec:disc-further-work}

Three directions are recommended for further work:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pump characterisation and optimisation.} The MHD pump's
    pressure--flow curve and efficiency should be measured, and the loop pressure
    drop reduced, to reach flows of at least \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}. A
    FEMM current-flow model of the pump was started in this project to guide its
    redesign, but it was not completed or validated, so it is not presented in
    the body of the report. The work to date, which suggests that longer
    electrodes, larger magnets and lower connection resistance would raise the
    pump efficiency
    considerably, is summarised in \cref{app:femm}.
  \item \textbf{Integrated power electronics.} The power electronics should be
    integrated into the pump design, to give a more accurate picture of the true
    pump size and total efficiency, including the power conversion needed to
    supply its high current at low voltage.
  \item \textbf{Reduced-size cold plate.} A smaller cold plate with an improved
    geometry, and a heated area below about \SI{40}{\centi\metre\squared},
    should be built to test the advantage predicted in \cref{sec:disc-area}.
\end{enumerate}
