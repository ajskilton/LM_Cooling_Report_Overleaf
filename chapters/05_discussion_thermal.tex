\section{Why Galinstan Is Caloric-Limited}
\label{sec:disc-caloric}

Galinstan's NTU was 35 to 65 in the tests, so $\varepsilon \approx 1$ and
$\Rcp \approx 1/(\mdot\,\cp)$. As a result, the coolant leaves the plate at almost the plate
temperature, and is therefore limited by the ability of the flow to store and transport heat energy.
Galinstan's high thermal conductivity gives a wall heat transfer coefficient of
about \SI{7700}{\watt\per\metre\squared\per\kelvin}, roughly 7.5 times that of
laminar water (\cref{app:convection}), but this advantage is not utilised with the current setup. 
The convective term $1/(hA)$, where galinstan has an advantage, accounts for less than \SI{3}{\percent} of $\Rcp$.
Water, by contrast, has $\NTU \approx 0.3$--0.4 at every tested flow, so
convection makes up about \SI{85}{\percent} of its $\Rcp$.

\Cref{fig:disc-regimes}(a) extends this over the full flow range. Galinstan
remains caloric-limited ($\NTU > 3$) up to the edge of the laminar region.
In contrast, water stays close to the convection-limited boundary above about
\SI{10}{\milli\litre\per\second}. For a caloric-limited coolant, the volumetric heat capacity is 
$\rho\cp$ the most important quantity, and here water has the
advantage (\cref{tab:disc-properties}): it absorbs about 1.8 times more heat per
unit volume per degree.

\section{Validity of the Measurements and Models}
\label{sec:disc-validity}

The conclusions above rest on two Galinstan tests and four water tests, and
their limitations should be weighed. Three points stand out.

First, the low-flow water results are limited by the flow measurement rather
than by the model. The measured $T_{\mathrm{M}} - \Tin$ stays between
\SI{16.1}{\celsius} and \SI{16.6}{\celsius} from \SIrange{60}{8.3}{\milli\litre\per\second},
and the \SI{0.5}{\litre\per\minute} point lies below water's caloric limit
(\cref{fig:combined_rcp}). A cold plate cannot have $\Rcp < 1/(\mdot\,\cp)$, so
the true flow in that test must have been at least \SI{9.6}{\milli\litre\per\second},
about \SI{15}{\percent} above the reading. This supports the conclusion that the
flow meter under-reads below its rated range and that these tests were not
laminar.

Second, $\Rpack$ was calibrated on the turbulent water tests, where the plate is
close to isothermal. Galinstan's coolant temperature rise of
17--\SI{18}{\celsius} violates this assumption: the outlet end of the plate runs
hotter than the mean, and the inferred $\Rpack$ for Galinstan ranges from
\SIrange{0.19}{0.24}{\celsius\per\watt} (isothermal plate) to
\SIrange{0.33}{0.51}{\celsius\per\watt} (uniform flux). The full-flow Galinstan point lying slightly below its caloric
limit is consistent with this. The lumped model therefore describes Galinstan
less reliably than water, although the agreement within the measurement
uncertainty (\cref{sec:results}) shows it captures the dominant behaviour.

Third, the conjugate heat transfer simulations address the main weakness of the
lumped model. They are mesh-independent above \num{427e3} cells and reproduce
all four probe temperatures within \SI{1.4}{\celsius} for both Galinstan tests.
The consistent over-prediction of the right-hand resistor by about
\SI{1.3}{\celsius} in both runs suggests a small systematic asymmetry in the
model, such as the heat-load distribution, rather than random error. The CFD
model can therefore be used with reasonable confidence to explore geometries and
flows that the test rig could not reach.

\section{Where Galinstan Is Best Suited}
\label{sec:disc-suitability}

The NTU analysis gives a clear criterion: Galinstan is advantageous where the
cooling surface, rather than the coolant flow, limits heat removal; that is,
where water would be convection-limited. \Cref{fig:disc-regimes}(b) illustrates
this using the validated $\varepsilon$--NTU model. The heated area is scaled by a
factor $f$ while the flow rate and heat transfer coefficient are held at their
values in the tested cold plate, so that $\NTU$ falls in proportion to $f$ and
$\Rcp = 1/[\mdot\,\cp\,(1 - e^{-f\,\NTU_0})]$, where $\NTU_0$ is the value for
the full plate. This represents concentrating the same heat into a smaller
footprint or a shorter heated length; the method and its assumptions are given in
\cref{app:area-scaling}. Reducing the heated area at
fixed flow raises water's $\Rcp$ almost in proportion, because water is already
close to convection-limited. At the tested flow, Galinstan's $\Rcp$ barely changes until
the area is reduced to about a fifth, because its excess $hA$ is simply used up. At
\SI{5}{\percent} of the tested area, Galinstan at \SI{10}{\milli\litre\per\second}
($\Rcp \approx \SI{0.12}{\celsius\per\watt}$) outperforms water at three times
the flow ($\Rcp \approx \SI{0.46}{\celsius\per\watt}$). Galinstan's resistance only begins to rise once
$f\,\NTU_0$ falls to about 3, so a coolant that starts with a large NTU can
absorb a large reduction in area before its performance suffers. Because $h$ is
held fixed and pressure drop is not considered, the result is indicative only,
but it shows the type of application in which Galinstan's conductivity becomes
useful.

Galinstan is therefore best suited to applications combining: (i) high heat
flux over a small area, such as direct cooling of power semiconductors or local
hot spots, or minichannel and microchannel heat sinks; (ii) a pump able to
deliver flow rates comparable to water, for which a sealed electromagnetic pump
with no moving parts may offer a reliability advantage; (iii) component
temperatures near or above \SI{100}{\celsius}, where water would boil without
pressurisation; and (iv) locations away from strong alternating magnetic fields.
\Cref{tab:disc-suitability} applies these criteria to the main components of an
IPT system.

These findings agree with Hodes et al.~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}, who found
that Galinstan's low volumetric heat capacity makes its caloric resistance a
larger share of the total than for water (\SI{55}{\percent} against
\SI{40}{\percent} in their optimised minichannel), and that its advantage depends
on geometries that exploit its high conductivity. The larger caloric share found
here (above \SI{97}{\percent}) reflects a cold plate designed for water and a
flow rate far below their optimum, rather than a contradiction of their result.

\section{Limitations and Recommendations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The main limitations of this study are the small number of valid Galinstan
tests, the uncertain flow measurement for both coolants at low flow, the
water-calibrated $\Rpack$, and the use of a single cold plate designed for
water. The comparison has also been made at equal volumetric flow rather than at
equal pumping power, which would penalise Galinstan further for its higher
density and viscosity.

Further work should:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item compare the coolants at equal pumping power, using the validated CFD
    model to obtain pressure drop as well as temperature;
  \item test Galinstan at higher flow rates, closer to
    \SIrange{10}{30}{\milli\litre\per\second}, with an independently calibrated
    flow meter;
  \item use the CFD model to evaluate small-area, high-flux geometries such as
    minichannel cold plates, where \cref{fig:disc-regimes}(b) indicates
    Galinstan may outperform water;
  \item quantify eddy-current heating and MHD drag through coupled
    electromagnetic--thermal analysis before Galinstan is placed near the IPT
    coupler; and
  \item assess long-term material compatibility, since gallium alloys attack
    aluminium and slowly alloy with copper.
\end{enumerate}
