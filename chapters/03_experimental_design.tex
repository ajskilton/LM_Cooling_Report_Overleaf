\chapter{Methodology}

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{ch:experimental}

This chapter details the experiments used to evaluate magnetohydrodynamically
pumped Galinstan as a thermal management method for PECs. The primary objective
is to compare the thermal performance of the Galinstan cooling system with that
of a similar water cooling system under equivalent operating conditions (RQ1).
A secondary objective is to quantify the influence of high-frequency magnetic
leakage fields on the thermal performance of the Galinstan coolant (RQ2).
\Cref{sec:programme} describes the experimental programme,
\cref{sec:inductor-design} the design of the inductor used in the second
experiment, and \cref{sec:thermal-model} the analytical thermal model against
which the measurements are compared.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experimental Preprogramme}
\label{sec:programme}

To answer the two research questions, two experimental methodologies are conducted. For safety reasons, experiments are to be set up by two project members, and the apparatus is to be continuously monitored by at least one project member whilst temperatures exceed \SI{36}{\celsius}. Experiment 1 compares the thermal performance of the water and Galinstan cooling systems across a range of flow rates. The data collected will then be used to build and validate thermal models that inform the application of Galinstan cooling in PECs. Experiment 2 demonstrates Galinstan’s ability to cool a power-dense inductor and quantifies the additional eddy-current loss induced in the coolant by the inductor’s magnetic field.

\subsubsection{Experiment 1: Baseline Thermal Comparison}
\label{sec:exp1}

The first experiment establishes a comparison of the thermal performance of the two cooling systems 
and is run on both systems using identical procedures. A water cooling system was designed to achieve this, details in my
partner’s report~\cite{ekbote_project_2026}. The goal is to replicate the Galinstan apparatus previously constructed by the 2025
Part IV Project Group \#51~\cite{kwon_project_2025, loh_project_2025}. 

For this experiment, heat is applied using two \SI{100}{\watt} \SI{25}{\ohm} ARCOL resistors.
The planned experiments are given in \cref{tab:flow-rates}. 
Later, two experiments at lower flow rates were conducted on the water-cooling system.
To do this, a valve was installed to reduce flow below the pump's controllable range.
Using the data provided by these experiments, a thermal resistance network can be established for each system.

\subsubsection{Experiment 2: Inductor Cooling}
\label{sec:exp2}

The second experiment demonstrates the ability of Galinstan to cool a
power-dense inductor, designed as described in \cref{sec:inductor-design}. The
procedure is identical to that of the baseline experiment, except that the
inductor replaces the resistive heat source. The inductor is driven at its rated
current to provide the heat load; its power loss at rated current is estimated in~\cref{sec:model-validation}. The Galinstan is
operated at maximum flow. Interaction between the magnetic leakage field and
the electrically conductive coolant is then assessed: the eddy-current loss
induced in the coolant is quantified by comparing LCR measurements taken on an
empty cold plate and on the cold plate filled with Galinstan; additionally, any change in
flow is captured by the flow sensor. For these experiments, an inverter and a 
resonant tank will be required to drive the required resonant voltage. 
All exposed terminals must be covered with acrylic so they cannot be accidentally contacted.

\subsubsection{Safety Considerations}
The safety of project participants and other lab members was prioritised during experimental setup and operation.
Specific measures included covering all electrical terminals operating above \SI{50}{\Volt}dc, a complete covering 
of the inverter and resonant tank setup required with acrylic boxes, protective eye-wear and 
physical barriers to prevent accidental interaction with the live system. Additionally, all setup was completed and 
cross-checked with a project partner before the system was allowed to be operated, and no interaction was permitted whilst in operation.

\subsection{Inductor Design for Experimentation}
\label{sec:inductor-design}

In high-power-density converters, magnetics are typically cooled from
both the top and the bottom, as close as possible to the
windings~\cite{krismer_accurate_2010,biela_cooling_2007}. Such cooling systems are
therefore exposed to leakage fields from the magnetic components, and these
fields could cause local heating in Galinstan, reducing the overall performance
of a liquid metal system.

An initial ANSYS simulation, in which the Galinstan was modelled as a stationary
solid, found that Galinstan was 3.2 times as lossy as aluminium and almost 3.9
times as lossy as copper. The same simulation showed that the eddy-current loss
in the coolant could be reduced by adding copper or aluminium shielding, with
\SI{1}{\milli\metre} of copper shielding reducing the total loss by over
\SI{70}{\percent}.

To evaluate the extent of this effect and possible mitigation strategies, a
power-dense inductor was built for experimentation. The inductor, which requires
active cooling, is placed on the Galinstan cold plate, and any increased loss due 
to the presence of the Galinstan system is assessed via LCR measurements. 
A planar geometry was selected for its better thermal performance than other geometries, 
such as pot cores. The final construction is shown in \cref{fig:inductor-photo}, 
and the design process is described in
Sections~\ref{sec:analytical-design}--\ref{sec:capacitor-tuning}.

\subsubsection{Analytical Design}
\label{sec:analytical-design}

To reduce computation time and narrow the design space, an analytical model was
developed before the numerical simulations. This initial model assumed a
two-part construction of TDK N85 ferrite ELP-type cores. It swept the air-gap
length (\SIrange{0.5}{1.5}{\milli\metre}) and the number of turns (8--12). This model
identifies designs whose loss exceeds the core's datasheet loss limit of \SI{17.88}{\watt}~\cite{magnetics_inc_specification_2005}, 
so that active cooling is required, while keeping the peak flux density below about
\SI{300}{\milli\tesla}.

An ANSYS model was then constructed and run as a two-parameter sweep over
air-gap length and number of turns. It was later found that the available cores
were Magnetics Inc.\ Material~R rather than the assumed TDK N85. A custom
material definition was therefore created by modifying the properties of TDK
N95, which are similar to those of Material~R. The core material was assumed to
operate at \SI{65}{\celsius}, the target steady-state operating temperature for
the experiments. With the material defined, a parameter sweep (8--12 turns,
\SIrange{0.5}{1.2}{\milli\metre} air gap) was performed to extract the
inductance and core loss.

To finalise the design, the flux density in the core (\cref{fig:flux-density})
was checked and deemed acceptable, within the linear region of operation. The
final design parameters are given in \cref{tab:inductor-design}, and the
inductance and core loss of the chosen design are given in
\cref{tab:inductor-model}.

\subsubsection{Model Validation}
\label{sec:model-validation}

Once the inductor had been built (\cref{fig:inductor-photo}), measurements were
taken to design the LCC tuning required to drive the desired resonant current;
These measurements also serve to validate the model. The model values at the
rated current of \SI{12}{\ampere} rms are given in \cref{tab:inductor-model}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.42\linewidth]{inductor_photo}
  \caption{Constructed planar inductor.}
  \label{fig:inductor-photo}

The inductance was measured as \SI{92.1}{\micro\henry}, within \SI{1}{\percent}
of the simulation model, although this measurement included the effect of long
leads, which would be expected to increase it. The AC resistance
$R_{\mathrm{ac}}$, which determines the winding loss, was measured with an LCR
meter at low excitation as \SI{83}{\milli\ohm}, giving a winding loss of
\SI{11.95}{\watt} at \SI{12}{\ampere} rms.

For a heat transfer investigation in which temperatures are continuously
monitored, some uncertainty in the loss estimate is acceptable. For later
experiments, the total inductor loss is therefore taken as \SI{42.76}{\watt}
(\SI{30.81}{\watt} core loss plus \SI{11.95}{\watt} winding loss).

\subsubsection{Thermal Considerations and Cooling Strategy}
\label{sec:inductor-thermal}

The main constraint was asymmetric cooling: the upper core surface relies on
convection to ambient air, while the lower surface interfaces with the cold
plate, the air gap acting as a thermal insulator between them. Thermal paste
was applied between the upper and lower cores to reduce the temperature
difference and improve heat transfer to the cooling system. Although not
optimal, this configuration is suitable for the second experiment, which aims to
demonstrate Galinstan cooling of a power-dense inductor and to assess the
leakage-field interaction rather than to produce the best-performing system.
Future work could improve this design by cooling both the top and bottom cores,
or by using asymmetric core designs with an integrated heat-transfer path, such
as the inductor of Gao \emph{et al.}~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.

\subsubsection{Capacitor Tuning}
\label{sec:capacitor-tuning}

The allowable reactance of the inductor was limited by the maximum voltage
developed across the series tuning capacitors needed to boost the volt-amperes
(VA) in the inductor. The allowable voltage was set to about \SI{130}{\volt},
with a maximum of \SI{150}{\volt} across any single capacitor. To find
acceptable tuning values, an optimisation model was developed using the Solver
add-in in Microsoft Excel, which made it possible to check quickly whether a
given inductance was feasible or would cause the capacitor voltage to exceed its
rating.

The target capacitance is given by
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{target}} = \frac{1}{2\pi f \left(2\pi f L_{1} - X_{\mathrm{res}}\right)},
  \label{eq:c-target}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the resonant frequency of the network, $L_{1}$ is the inductance of
the primary magnetics and $X_{\mathrm{res}}$ is the resonant reactance of the
system. $X_{\mathrm{res}}$ sets the resonant current in the primary magnetics,
as given by
\begin{equation}
  X_{\mathrm{res}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\, V_{\mathrm{DC}}}{I_{1}},
  \label{eq:x-res}
\end{equation}
where $I_{1}$ is the desired resonant current and $V_{\mathrm{DC}}$ is the DC bus
voltage. Note that \cref{eq:x-res} is valid only for a conduction angle of
\SI{180}{\degree}.

\section{Thermal Modelling}
\label{sec:thermal-model}

Thermal models are developed in parallel to experimentation to better analyse the performance of each system.
This section outlines the process of creating these models; comparisons will be presented in~\cref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Thermal Network}
\label{sec:thermal-network}

Following the analyses of Kwon~\cite{kwon_project_2025} and
Loh~\cite{loh_project_2025}, the input heat $\Qin$ divides at the resistor node
$T_{\mathrm{M}}$ (\cref{fig:thermal-network}): part is lost to ambient through
$\Rmisc$, and the rest, $\Qplate$, passes through the package resistance $\Rpack$
(resistor and interface) to a fictitious isothermal plate node $T_{\mathrm{P}}$,
and then through the cold-plate resistance $\Rcp$ to the coolant inlet:
\begin{equation}
  \Qin = \frac{T_{\mathrm{M}} - T_{\mathrm{A}}}{\Rmisc}
       + \frac{T_{\mathrm{M}} - \Tin}{\Rpack + \Rcp}.
  \label{eq:network}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{thermal_network}
  \caption{Thermal network as an electrical analogue (temperature $\equiv$
    potential, heat flow $\equiv$ current); $T_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\Tin$ are fixed
    boundary conditions.}
  \label{fig:thermal-network}
\end{figure}

This network was used in all the following models.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon$--NTU}{epsilon-NTU} Analysis of the Cold Plate}
\label{sec:eps-ntu}

For this analysis, the plate is considered isothermal at $T_{\mathrm{P}}$, so
coolant entering at $\Tin$ approaches the plate temperature exponentially. An
energy balance on a tube element of perimeter $P$ gives~\cite{bergman_fundamentals_2017}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{T_{\mathrm{P}} - T_{\mathrm{f}}(x)}{T_{\mathrm{P}} - \Tin}
    = \exp\!\left(-\frac{hPx}{\mdot\,\cp}\right).
  \label{eq:temp-profile}
\end{equation}
At the outlet ($x = L$, $PL = A$), the exponent is defined as the number of
transfer units, $\NTU = hA/(\mdot\,\cp)$: the ratio of the wall-to-coolant
convective conductance to the heat-capacity rate of the coolant. Each transfer
unit reduces the remaining plate-to-coolant temperature difference by a factor
of $e$, so the coolant absorbs about \SI{63}{\percent} of the maximum possible
heat after one transfer unit~\cite{bergman_fundamentals_2017}. Defining the
effectiveness $\varepsilon$ as this fraction gives:

\begin{equation}
  \Qplate = \varepsilon\,\mdot\,\cp\,(T_{\mathrm{P}} - \Tin), \qquad
  \varepsilon = 1 - e^{-\NTU}, \qquad
  \Rcp = \frac{1}{\varepsilon\,\mdot\,\cp},
  \label{eq:effectiveness}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{P}} = \Tin + \Qplate\Rcp, \qquad
  \Tout = \Tin + \frac{\Qplate}{\mdot\,\cp}.
  \label{eq:plate-outlet}
\end{equation}
This is the effectiveness relation for one flow stream against an isothermal surface~\cite{bergman_fundamentals_2017}.
Taking the limits of $\varepsilon$ shows whether a coolant is convection-limited
or limited by heat transport (caloric-limited), as summarised in
\cref{tab:ntu-regimes}. %and \cref{fig:eps-ntu}.

\subsubsection{Convection, Material Properties and Tests}
\label{sec:convection}

The $\varepsilon$--NTU model requires an estimate of the heat transfer
coefficient $h$ between the tube wall and the coolant. It is obtained from standard correlations for the Nusselt number,
$\mathrm{Nu} = hD/k$, a dimensionless measure of how effectively the flowing
coolant removes heat from the wall. For laminar flow, the Shah and London
correlation~\cite{shah_laminar_1978} gives the mean Nu along a tube at constant
wall temperature, including the thermal entry region where heat transfer is
highest. It is applied to each \SI{152}{\milli\metre} pass separately, on the
assumption that the U-bends mix the flow. For turbulent water
($\mathrm{Re} \geq 4000$), Gnielinski's correlation~\cite{bergman_fundamentals_2017}
is used. No estimate is made in the transitional range
$2300 < \mathrm{Re} < 4000$. Galinstan remains laminar up to
\SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second} ($\mathrm{Re} \leq 2110$). These concepts, the
correlations and worked examples for both coolants are given in
\cref{app:convection}.

The NTU analysis uses these correlations for both coolants. For the turbulent
water tests, however, the temperature predictions use the provided value of 
$\Rcp$ from the ATS-TCP-1001 datasheet~\cite{advanced_thermal_solutions_inc_tubed_2021}, fitted as
$\Rcp = 0.0274\,\Vdot^{-0.415}$~\si{\kelvin\per\watt} ($\Vdot$ in
\si{\litre\per\minute}); \SI{1.8}{\litre\per\minute} lies just below the lowest
datasheet point of \SI{1.89}{\litre\per\minute}. The Galinstan and laminar water
tests use the $\varepsilon$--NTU model. Galinstan remains laminar up to
\SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second} ($\mathrm{Re} \leq 2110$).
\Cref{tab:coolant-properties,tab:tests} summarise the cold-plate geometry, the
coolant properties and the test conditions. Full calculations are available in Appendix \cref{app:convection}.

\subsection{Finite Element Analysis}

The thermal network reproduces the measured resistor temperatures by lumping the
cold plate into a single isothermal node. For Galinstan, the plate is not isothermal.
The network also cannot resolve how heat spreads from discrete resistor
footprints through the press-fit tube contact. It cannot capture flow
redevelopment at the U-bends or the transitional flow suspected in the low-flow
water tests. A finite-element conjugate heat transfer simulation completed in SimScale~\cite{simscale_gmbh_simscale_2021} resolves the temperature fields in the plate and
coolant and the local peak temperatures that govern component and insulation
limits.

The simulation was set up as follows. First, to reduce computation time, a simplified plate model was created in OnShape~\cite{ptc_onshape_2015}. This model was then imported into the SimScale Software, the simulation was set up as in~\cref{tab:simulation-setup}. Parameters not present were left as their default.

Next, an internal flow volume was defined in the geometry. The model was populated with the default material parameters for aluminium, copper and water. A custom Galinstan material was prepared using the parameters specified in~\cref{tab:coolant-properties}. The boundary conditions were set to a pressure of 1~atm at the outlet, and the heat flux was set to \SI{60}{\watt} to account for the loss to the environment. Roughly \SI{12{\watt} is expected to be lost to the environment based on experiments completed in~\cite{kwon_project_2025, loh_project_2025} and those completed in parrallel to the preparation of this model. The velocity inlet temperature was set to \SI{29.8}{C}, and the velocity was adjusted to match the desired flow rate.

Turbulent cases were solved with the $k$--$\omega$ SST turbulence model; a laminar model was used for the runs with estimated $\mathrm{Re} \leq 2110$. Mesh independence was consequently assessed separately for the laminar and turbulent models for each coolant. Each study was performed at the most demanding operating point so that the resulting mesh is conservative for all milder cases. For any test, this is the highest flow, since the boundary region will be the most extreme. The same three systematically more refined meshes were used in each study. The studies were conducted independently for each coolant because Galinstan has a thermal boundary layer much thicker than its velocity boundary layer, whereas water has a thermal boundary layer thinner than its velocity boundary layer and therefore requires finer near-wall resolution for the temperature field.
