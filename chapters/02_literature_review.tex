The continued electrification of the global economy is driving a sustained
increase in electricity demand~\cite{international_energy_agency_electricity_2025},
and PECs enable this shift in transport~\cite{bosshard_multi-objective_2016} and
energy systems~\cite{steimer_enabled_2010}. Across all their applications, the
power density of PECs is increasing, driven by cost reduction, increased
functionality and, in automotive and aerospace applications, hard weight and
space limits~\cite{steimer_enabled_2010,biela_cooling_2007}. The trend has
persisted for decades, enabled largely by improvements in power-conversion
efficiency, and is projected to continue~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}.

PECs switch semiconductor devices at high frequency and use passive components,
chiefly inductors, transformers and capacitors, to store and release energy in
each switching cycle and to filter the resulting
waveforms~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}. Magnetic components, frequently the
largest passive devices, are difficult to scale and often restrict the
achievable power
density~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,morroni_understanding_2023,sullivan_size_2016};
with heat sinks, they account for the largest portion of a power solution's
size~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,morroni_understanding_2023}. Because less energy
must be stored per cycle as frequency rises, higher switching frequencies permit
smaller inductors~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}, and wide-bandgap devices
enable such frequencies~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. However, switching losses grow
with frequency~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}, as do winding and core
losses~\cite{ouyang_overview_2014}, so converter losses and thermal management
limit further increases in frequency and power
density~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}.

Shrinking components also reduces the area available for heat dissipation,
raising heat flux and worsening the thermal resistance of the
package~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}, so further size reduction requires
cooling methods that transfer more heat per unit area~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}.
As \cref{sec:thermal-management} shows, reliability depends on meeting this
challenge.

\section{Thermal Management in Power Electronics}
\label{sec:thermal-management}
\edited{Restructured: this section now makes only the reliability argument,
which ends with the two reliability requirements (temperature limit and
temperature swing). The paragraph on cooling-system volume and pumping power
moved to \cref{sec:cooling-strategies}, where all four key considerations are
listed together once and tabulated; previously the requirements were stated
three times (here, as a bullet list, and in the summary of cooling schemes).
The two magnetics subsections are merged into \cref{sec:cooling-magnetics}, and
``Trends in Electronics Cooling'' became \cref{sec:cooling-strategies}.}

Thermal management is required, above all, to keep increasingly power-dense
PECs reliable. Thermal stress is responsible for over \SI{50}{\percent} of
electronic equipment failures~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024}, and power
semiconductor devices, which account for approximately \SI{31}{\percent} of PEC
failures, more than any other component~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025},
significantly affect the reliability of the entire
system~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024,kalay_systematic_2025}.

One of the simplest lifetime models relating thermal stress to the number of
thermal cycles to failure of a semiconductor device is the Coffin--Manson
model~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025,albarbar_thermal_2018}:
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{f}} = \alpha \left(\Delta T_{\mathrm{j}}\right)^{\beta},
  \label{eq:coffin-manson}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the number of power cycles (in thousands) before
failure, $\Delta T_{\mathrm{j}}$ is the swing in junction temperature
$T_{\mathrm{j}}$, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are experimentally determined
parameters~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025}. This simplified form considers only the
temperature swing and omits the Arrhenius term for absolute temperature, which
becomes significant mainly above about \SI{200}{\celsius}~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025}.
\edited{Equation numbering in the draft started at (2.2) with no (2.1); equations
are now numbered automatically.}
The absolute temperature is nevertheless limited. The junction temperature of a
power semiconductor must be kept below its rated maximum, typically
\SIrange{125}{175}{\celsius} for silicon devices~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}, and
packaging limits the junction temperature of even silicon carbide (SiC) and
gallium nitride (GaN) devices to about \SI{175}{\celsius}~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024}.
\edited{The rated-maximum statement was cited to [Liu \emph{et al.}, wireless
charging]; Laloya \emph{et al.} is the source that states it (and the
\SIrange{125}{175}{\celsius} range), so the two citations were merged.
Grammar of the SiC/GaN clause fixed (``packaging limits even SiC \dots\ devices
limit'').}
Magnetic components are subject to similar
limits~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,mengesha_advanced_2025}; soft ferrites, for example,
have the lowest Curie temperature of the common soft magnetic
materials~\cite{li_review_2022}. Thermal management for high reliability must
therefore both hold the maximum temperature of semiconductors and magnetics
below these limits and suppress the temperature swing $\Delta T_{\mathrm{j}}$.

\subsection{Cooling in the Presence of Magnetic Components}
\label{sec:cooling-magnetics}

Magnetic components shape thermal management in two ways: they must themselves
be cooled, and their leakage fields act on any cooling hardware near them. The
design of highly power-dense magnetics requires additional consideration of
material selection and thermal management~\cite{li_review_2022}, and their
thermal design is becoming harder~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}: operating modes such as
zero-voltage switching (ZVS) and discontinuous conduction mode (DCM),
increasingly used at higher frequencies and power levels, reduce switching
losses and the required inductance but cause high inductor current ripple, and
hence substantial AC loss and temperature rise~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.
Gao \emph{et al.}\ identify temperature limits as a major constraint on such
inductors, argue that better thermal management is needed for further size
reduction, and note that effective solutions for high-power, high-density
planar magnetics (e.g.\ with ELP cores) are yet to be
developed~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.

Inductive power transfer (IPT) systems, such as those used for electric vehicle
(EV) charging, transfer power across a large air gap between loosely coupled
coils, and many commercial designs actively cool the coils, particularly on the
space- and weight-constrained vehicle side~\cite{lawton_power_2024}. Prolonged
or repeated charging can damage the coils and power electronics. Liu
\emph{et al.}\ therefore recommend research into technologies for cooling
wireless chargers~\cite{liu_thermal_2025}.

Magnetic components are conventionally cooled by convection from their exposed
surfaces, which is limited by the heat transfer coefficient of
air~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}. Planar constructions improve on this through
their high surface-area-to-volume ratio~\cite{gao_modeling_2022,ouyang_overview_2014},
but a flat cold plate often cools only one side of the core and falls short of
cooling the windings~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. Gao \emph{et al.}\ addressed this
with an engineered conduction path (a custom aluminium cold plate, ceramic shim,
aluminium bracket and thermal vias) that improved power
density~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.

Such hardware, however, sits in the component's leakage field. Soft ferrite
cores have a low saturation flux density, typically \SIrange{0.2}{0.5}{\tesla},
so an air gap is often required, leading to fringing
flux~\cite{li_review_2022} that can increase winding
losses~\cite{ouyang_overview_2014}. Conductive cooling hardware placed close to
the windings also suffers increased
losses~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,gao_modeling_2022}. In the design of Gao
\emph{et al.}, eddy currents induced in the metal raised the measured AC winding
resistance by \SI{34}{\percent}~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}, and Biela and Kolar
found that a heat-transfer component near an inductor air gap suffers
significant eddy-current loss and must be recessed from
it~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}.

Because magnetic components must be present in most
converters~\cite{morroni_understanding_2023}, their leakage fields must be
considered in thermal management, even where the magnetics are not the main
thermal constraint. In a compact converter, a cooling system
that does not directly cool the magnetics is still close to them, so it must
incur minimal loss in their fields and must not compromise the magnetics'
performance. This is particularly relevant to conductive coolants such as
liquid metal, to which \cref{sec:cooling-strategies} returns.

Besides meeting the reliability requirements of \cref{sec:thermal-management},
a PEC cooling system must not degrade the power density it is meant to enable.
In compact systems, up to half of the volume can be required for the cooling
system~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}, and the power consumed to move the coolant
must remain small relative to the power processed if efficiency is to be
maintained~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}. Four key considerations for PEC thermal
management therefore follow. The cooling system must (i)~hold the maximum
temperature of both semiconductors and magnetics below their operating limits,
(ii)~suppress the temperature swing, (iii)~be compact and (iv)~draw minimal
power. Many schemes have been developed to handle increasingly high heat
fluxes~\cite{liu_thermal_2025,rahman_emerging_2024,laloya_heat_2016,mudawar_assessment_2001};
\cref{tab:cooling-strategies} compares the main single-phase and phase-change
schemes against these considerations.

Single-phase liquid cooling meets more of these considerations than air, but
the thermal conductivity $k$ of water is low, about
\SI{0.6}{\watt\per\metre\per\kelvin}~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. In fully
developed laminar flow the Nusselt number is constant, so the heat transfer
coefficient scales with $k/D_{\mathrm{h}}$ and can be raised only by shrinking
the hydraulic diameter $D_{\mathrm{h}}$~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Narrower
channels, however, raise the pump power (\cref{tab:cooling-strategies}), and
below a practical minimum diameter the likelihood of clogging increases
drastically~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}. Water therefore trades heat flux
(consideration~(i)) against pumping power (consideration~(iv)).
\edited{This citation was highlighted in the draft. Checked: Mudawar (2001)
does state that the likelihood of clogging ``increases very drastically'' at
small hydraulic diameters, so the citation is correct and the highlight was
removed.}

Despite their higher heat transfer rates, phase-change systems are complex to
implement~\cite{laloya_heat_2016,mudawar_assessment_2001}. The central
difficulty is the critical heat flux (CHF). Beyond it, vapour restricts liquid
access to the heated surface and the device temperature rises uncontrollably, so
CHF is the upper design limit for any phase-change
system~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}. Because CHF depends on mass velocity,
subcooling, pressure, hydraulic diameter, heated length and surface
orientation~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}, it is difficult to predict, and the
required safety margin erodes this advantage.

Of the established strategies, a single-phase forced-liquid loop best meets the
key considerations, because a controlled flow rate can suppress temperature
swings, which passive phase-change methods cannot
(\cref{tab:cooling-strategies}).

\section{Liquid Metal as a Coolant}
\label{sec:liquid-metal}

Liquid metals have long been used as coolants where heat fluxes and operating
temperatures exceed those that conventional fluids can
tolerate~\cite{rowinski_small_2015}. Much of the early characterisation of liquid
metal cooling systems was carried out as fundamental research for nuclear
reactors, including small modular reactors (SMRs)~\cite{rowinski_small_2015},
which produce on the order of tens to hundreds of megawatts. These systems are
now at an advanced stage of research, with the switch to liquid metals driven
largely by their high-temperature stability. SMRs typically use lead or sodium
coolants, but lead is toxic and requires careful handling and
containment~\cite{oecd_handbook_2015}, whereas sodium is highly chemically
reactive, particularly with water and air, posing fire and explosion
hazards~\cite{foust_sodium-nak_1972}. These factors complicate broader commercial
deployment.
\issue{Historically, most liquid-metal coolant characterisation was done for

what Rowinski \emph{et al.} actually support.}

With the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) and electrification, liquid metal
has more recently been proposed as a coolant for computing hardware and
PECs~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026,zhang_water-based_2015}. The driving factor is its high thermal
conductivity, which promises high heat transfer
rates~\cite{hodes_potential_2014,fan_ultra-efficient_2026,zhang_experimental_2019,zhang_investigation_2023}.
Such applications typically use gallium (Ga)-based eutectic alloys, the most
common being Galinstan (GaInSn), which are non-toxic and have low melting points,
remaining liquid at room temperature~\cite{hodes_potential_2014,an_liquid_2024}.

Galinstan has a thermal conductivity of \SI{16.5}{\watt\per\metre\per\kelvin},
approximately 28 times that of water (\cref{tab:galinstan-properties}), which
enhances heat spreading and reduces temperature
gradients~\cite{hodes_potential_2014,zhang_experimental_2019}. Its boiling point
above \SI{1300}{\celsius} means that boiling will not occur when it is used to
cool electronics~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}, and its melting point is below room
temperature, so it is liquid under ambient conditions, although the reported
melting point depends on alloy composition and ranges from
\SI{-19}{\celsius}~\cite{hodes_potential_2014} to
\SI{10}{\celsius}~\cite{zhang_experimental_2019}. A Galinstan loop is therefore
single-phase, with no boiling limit to design around, and has the potential to
remove a higher heat flux than water.

These benefits come with significant trade-offs (\cref{tab:galinstan-properties}).
Galinstan is electrically conductive, more than six times denser and 2.4 times
as viscous as water, and has roughly one-fourteenth of its specific heat
capacity. In a previous Part~IV project, Kwon used \GaInSn{68.5}{21.5}{10} with a
density of \SI{6370}{\kilogram\per\metre\cubed} and a specific heat capacity of
\SI{360}{\joule\per\kilogram\per\kelvin}~\cite{kwon_project_2025}, which gives
about 0.55 of water's volumetric heat capacity. Many of these properties depend
on the alloy~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Galinstan is therefore heavier, harder to
pump and requires higher flow rates.

Much of the reviewed work on Galinstan and other Ga-based coolants focuses on
micro- and mini-channel heat sinks for electronics, typically for heat loads
from watts to hundreds of watts. It is unclear from the literature whether such
systems can be scaled up to the channel sizes and temperatures more typical of
PECs. For example, EV-charging PECs have power classes on the order of tens of
kilowatts for light-duty vehicles and are being standardised up to
\SI{1}{\mega\watt} for heavy-duty
vehicles~\cite{lawton_power_2024,liu_thermal_2025,abb_e-mobility_mcs1200_nodate}. Large liquid
metal systems have been realised for nuclear reactors~\cite{rowinski_small_2015},
but these use different metals, operate at much higher temperatures and are much
larger than PEC applications, although some work has addressed power electronic
converters~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026,fan_liquid_2021}.

\subsection{Electromagnetic Pumping}
\label{sec:em-pumping}

The low volumetric heat capacity of Galinstan makes the pumping requirement more
demanding. For a given heat load $Q$ and coolant temperature rise $\Delta T$,
the required volumetric flow rate is
\begin{equation}
  \Vdot = \frac{Q}{\rho \, \cp \, \Delta T},
  \label{eq:flow-requirement}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the coolant density and $\cp$ its specific heat capacity. With
0.55 of the volumetric heat capacity of water~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
Galinstan needs about 1.8 times the flow rate of water for the same temperature
rise and heat load. The coolant temperature rise per unit heat load,
$\Delta T/Q = 1/(\rho\,\cp\,\Vdot)$, is known as the caloric (or heat-capacity)
resistance, which adds to the convective resistance at the heated surface. In
the optimised heat sinks reported by Hodes \emph{et al.}, caloric resistance
accounts for about \SI{55}{\percent} of the total thermal resistance for
Galinstan, compared with \SI{40}{\percent} for water~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
and An \emph{et al.}\ identify caloric resistance as the primary bottleneck for
liquid metal micro- and mini-channel heat sinks~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. The high
thermal conductivity partly offsets this, as it yields higher heat transfer
coefficients in wider channels~\cite{zhang_water-based_2015,an_liquid_2024}. However,
liquid metal requires more pumping power than water at the same volume flow
because of its higher viscosity~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Pumps for electronics
cooling are generally reported as
individual designs~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026,fan_liquid_2021,hart_magnetohydrodynamically_2025},
with only~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026} providing a comparison with an equivalent water
system.

made at the chip scale, in mini- or micro-channels'' was cut: the same point is
made at the end of \cref{sec:liquid-metal} and is the first research gap.}
\issue{0.55/1.8$\times$ are Kwon's numbers but are cited to Hodes, who gives
0.45/2.2$\times$ (and \cref{tab:galinstan-properties} uses 0.45/2.2$\times$).
Make the number and the citation agree.}
\edited{The 55\,\%/40\,\% split is Hodes' result only, so the sentence was
restructured so that An \emph{et al.} is cited for the ``primary bottleneck''
point rather than for the percentages.}
\issue{``Only~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026} provides a comparison with an equivalent water
system'' conflicts with the end of the next paragraph, which reports (via An
\emph{et al.}) a conduction pump compared against a water-cooling mechanical
pump. Qualify this claim (e.g.\ ``at converter scale'') or remove it.}

The electrical conductivity of Galinstan allows it to be driven by
electromagnetic (EM) pumps with no moving parts. These pumps are silent,
vibration-free and compact~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. The EM pump built in a
previous Part~IV project is a permanent-magnet DC conduction
pump~\cite{kwon_project_2025,loh_project_2025}, although pumps can also be based on
electromagnetic induction~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. In a conduction pump, a current
passed through the conductive fluid, perpendicular to a magnetic field, produces
a Lorentz force. The driving force, and therefore the flow rate, can be
increased by increasing the magnetic flux density or the driving
current~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Flow is usually adjusted by varying the driving
current~\cite{an_liquid_2024}, which allows the cooling to be modulated and
temperature swings to be suppressed. For electronics cooling, conduction pumps
typically operate at high current (\SIrange{1}{100}{\ampere}) and low voltage
(\SIrange{0.01}{0.5}{\volt}), consuming approximately
\SIrange{0.1}{50}{\watt}~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Their efficiency is low: up to
about \SI{10}{\percent} for a pump reported in~\cite{hodes_potential_2014} and
above \SI{6}{\percent} over most flow rates for a pump reviewed
in~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Even so, a conduction pump consumed less than
\SI{15}{\percent} of the power of a mechanical water pump at the same heating
power and cooling performance~\cite{an_liquid_2024}.
\edited{The current/voltage/power ranges were cited to Zhang \emph{et al.}
(2015); they come from An \emph{et al.} (2024), so the citation was corrected.
``only 15\,\%'' changed to ``less than 15\,\%'': An \emph{et al.} report
\SI{1.5}{\watt} against ``more than \SI{10}{\watt}''.}

Induction pumps instead induce current in the liquid metal through an
alternating or travelling magnetic field. They are more complex and larger, but
can produce greater driving forces~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Furthermore, the
magnetic field can be derived directly from the converter. Fan \emph{et al.}\
integrated a magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) pump into an inductor so that it pumps
the liquid metal while remaining part of the converter~\cite{fan_liquid_2021}.
Because the inductor is part of the converter being cooled, the flow rate
self-adjusts with converter loading~\cite{fan_liquid_2021}. As the field is
produced by the converter current, the coolant is exposed to alternating fields
and therefore to eddy-current losses~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026}.

In summary, permanent-magnet DC conduction pumps are usually preferred for
electronics cooling because of their simple construction and compact size.
Electromagnetic pumping, despite its low electrical-to-hydraulic efficiency,
provides effective heat transport at low pumping power.

\subsection{Compatibility with Electrical Equipment}
\label{sec:compatibility}

The use of Galinstan in commercial settings requires a high level of user safety
and compatibility with the electrical equipment. Three key concerns arise when
a new material is introduced into these environments: material safety, corrosion
and electromagnetic compatibility.

\subsubsection*{Material Safety}

Galinstan was developed as a non-toxic, non-flammable replacement for mercury in
thermometry~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Ga-based alloys are low-melting-point
metals that avoid the toxicity of mercury, the flammability of NaK
(sodium--potassium alloy) and the radioactivity of francium~\cite{an_liquid_2024}.
Galinstan's vapour pressure is negligible even at high temperatures, so it poses
no inhalation risk~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Galinstan does not pose a major
hazard, although long-term exposure to gallium oxide should be
avoided~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Gallium oxide can damage the respiratory
system at high airborne concentrations, but only minimal amounts are released
from a closed liquid metal loop~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Prior prototypes managed
risks, including skin contact and spillage, using nitrile gloves, eye protection
and containment trays~\cite{kwon_project_2025,loh_project_2025}.

\subsubsection*{Corrosion and Chemical Compatibility}

Galinstan is widely described as corrosive to metals~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
although An \emph{et al.}\ report that Ga-based alloys are generally unreactive,
except that they corrode aluminium severely and copper surfaces require
protection above \SI{100}{\celsius}~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. This presents a
potential challenge for PEC hardware, as many conventional designs use aluminium
for structural components, heat sinks or electromagnetic shielding. Moreover, the
effect of Galinstan exposure on the mechanical properties of ductile metals
(e.g.\ aluminium) remains to be evaluated~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. The high
velocities required to reduce the caloric resistance also raise concern about
erosion, which has yet to be studied~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Plastics or
nickel-plated metals address both corrosion and surface
oxide~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}, and ceramic substrates such as aluminium nitride
are compatible with Ga-based alloys~\cite{an_liquid_2024} while being both
thermally conductive and electrically insulating.
\edited{Sentence fragment (``Although oxidation is severe on aluminium \dots'')
merged into the previous sentence. An \emph{et al.} describe the effect on
aluminium as \emph{corrosion}, not oxidation, so the wording follows the
source.}

\subsubsection*{Electrical Hazards and Electromagnetic Compatibility}

With a conductivity of about \SI{3.3e6}{\siemens\per\metre}~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
Galinstan is a conductor wherever it flows. Its high surface tension resists
seepage through small cracks at moderate pressure, but a leak onto energised
circuitry is a direct short, and the electronics must be de-energised before
clean-up~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. The loop also galvanically connects every
wetted metal part, so a cooling system design must consider how the EM pump is
energised, as the pump supply can also energise any metallic radiator and cold
plate~\cite{kwon_project_2025}.

As anticipated in \cref{sec:cooling-magnetics}, AC fields from nearby
magnetics induce eddy currents in the conductive
coolant~\cite{an_liquid_2024}.
\edited{``As anticipated in \cref{sec:cooling-magnetics}'' added so that the
eddy-current point reads as a callback to the merged magnetics subsection
rather than as a new point.}
The skin depth in Galinstan,
$\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f \mu_0 \sigma}$, calculated from the conductivity above,
is about \SI{0.95}{\milli\metre} at \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz} and about
\SI{0.55}{\milli\metre} at \SI{250}{\kilo\hertz}. Given Galinstan's
incompatibility with many metals, plastic piping may be preferred for low cost;
near magnetics, where alternating magnetic fields are present, nickel-plated
metals may be a better choice to manage eddy-current heating. Channel placement
and geometry may therefore govern the choice of piping material.
\edited{Skin-depth expression added so the quoted values can be checked
(they are correct for $\sigma = \SI{3.3e6}{\siemens\per\metre}$).}
\issue{Nickel is ferromagnetic, so a nickel-plated pipe in an \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz}

\section{Research Gap and Objectives}
\label{sec:research-gap}

\subsection{Research Gap}
\edited{Trimmed only. The two opening sentences (water cooling is bounded by
water's thermal conductivity; Galinstan's higher conductivity can improve heat
transfer, both cited to Hodes \emph{et al.}) repeated
\cref{sec:cooling-strategies,sec:liquid-metal} and were cut. The two closing sentences of the second gap (most converters

This study aims to address two gaps identified in the literature.

The first is the lack of comparisons between water and liquid metal at the scale
of a converter's cooling loop. Existing comparisons are made for micro- and
mini-channel heat
sinks~\cite{hodes_potential_2014,zhang_water-based_2015,zhang_experimental_2019,an_liquid_2024},
or lie at the scale of nuclear reactors, which use different metals at much
higher temperatures~\cite{rowinski_small_2015}. This study aims to quantify the
trade-offs of the Galinstan system (as designed
in~\cite{kwon_project_2025,loh_project_2025}) relative to a comparable water-cooling
system.

The second is the cooling of magnetic components with Galinstan. To the author's
knowledge, no study has demonstrated Galinstan cooling of a magnetic component,
such as a power-dense inductor, or quantified the eddy-current losses induced in
the coolant by its leakage field. Eddy-current losses have been assessed in AC
conduction pumps~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026}, and an inductor has been used to drive a
pump~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026,fan_liquid_2021}, but not to cool the inductor itself
directly. As argued in \cref{sec:cooling-magnetics}, this matters even where
the coolant does not cool the magnetics.

This project addresses both gaps using the existing Galinstan loop of
Loh~\cite{loh_project_2025} and Kwon~\cite{kwon_project_2025}. The loop comprises a
permanent-magnet MHD pump, perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) tubing, a cold plate and a
fan-cooled radiator, and can remove up to a \SI{72}{\watt} resistive
load~\cite{kwon_project_2025,loh_project_2025}. The loop was not tested against water
in the same apparatus, and its experiments did not consider magnetics:
Loh~\cite{loh_project_2025} focused on switching devices because Galinstan was
unlikely to interfere with them, unlike magnetics, and Kwon~\cite{kwon_project_2025}
excluded direct integration and testing with magnetics because of time
limitations and uncertainty about electromagnetic interference. Both recommended
comparison with water cooling, and Kwon also recommended testing higher heat
loads and investigating the interaction with inductive pad
fields~\cite{kwon_project_2025,loh_project_2025}.

project's loop, not this project; reworded to ``The loop was not tested
\dots''.}
