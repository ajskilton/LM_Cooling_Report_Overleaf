\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The continued electrification of the global economy is driving a sustained
increase in electricity demand~\cite{noauthor_electricity_2025}. Advances in power
electronics are enabling this shift, underpinning the electrification of
transport~\cite{bosshard_multi-objective_2016} and energy systems~\cite{steimer_enabled_2010}.
Across all applications of PECs, there is a consistent trend toward higher power
densities, driven by cost reduction, increased functionality and, in automotive
and aerospace applications, hard weight and space
limits~\cite{steimer_enabled_2010,biela_cooling_2007}.

The trend toward higher power density has been present in the industry for
decades and is projected to continue~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}.
Historically, this progress has largely been enabled by fundamental improvements
in power-conversion efficiency, which allow the solution size of converters to
shrink~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}. Higher switching frequencies allow
passive components to shrink; however, switching losses grow with switching
frequency~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}. Converter losses and thermal
management therefore limit further increases in frequency and, with them, power
density~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}.

To achieve higher power density, component volume must be reduced. However,
reduced volume also reduces the surface area available for heat dissipation:
heat flux rises, and the thermal resistance of the package
worsens~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}. Consequently, new cooling methods that
can transfer more heat per unit area are required to reduce the size of PECs
further~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}. Thermal management therefore becomes
progressively more challenging for high-power-density PECs, and this is the
problem that the cooling approaches reviewed in this chapter attempt to address.
The difficulty of scaling magnetic components, in particular, often restricts
the achievable power
density~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,morroni_understanding_nodate,sullivan_size_2016}.

\section{Power Electronics}
\label{sec:power-electronics}

PECs process energy by switching semiconductor devices at high frequency, and
rely on passive components, chiefly inductors, transformers and capacitors, to
store and release energy in each switching cycle and to filter the resulting
waveforms~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}. Magnetic components (transformers and
inductors) are often the largest passive devices and limit the achievable size
of the converter~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,morroni_understanding_nodate,sullivan_size_2016}.

Because less energy must be stored per switching cycle as frequency rises,
higher switching frequencies permit smaller
inductors~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}. Wide-bandgap devices enable higher
switching frequencies and, therefore, smaller magnetics~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.
Even so, inductors, transformers and heat sinks account for the largest portion
of a power solution's size~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,morroni_understanding_nodate}.
Moreover, raising the frequency increases winding and core
losses~\cite{ouyang_overview_2014}. As a result, the design of highly power-dense
magnetics requires additional consideration of material selection and thermal
management~\cite{li_review_2022}.

Inductive power transfer (IPT) systems, such as those used for electric vehicle
(EV) charging, transfer power across a large air gap between loosely coupled
coils. Thermal limits shape the magnetic and electronic design of these systems.
Many commercial systems use active cooling on the magnetic coils, particularly on
the space- and weight-constrained vehicle side~\cite{lawton_power_2024}. As in many
other PEC applications, prolonged or repeated charging can damage the coils and
power electronics. Liu \emph{et al.}\ therefore recommend research into
technologies for cooling wireless chargers~\cite{liu_thermal_2025}.

\section{Thermal Management in Power Electronics}
\label{sec:thermal-management}

Thermal management is driven primarily by the requirement for high reliability
in PECs. Thermal stress is responsible for over \SI{50}{\percent} of electronic
equipment failures~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024}. In power converters, power
semiconductor devices are the components most responsible for failure,
accounting for approximately \SI{31}{\percent} of PEC
failures~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025}. Power semiconductor devices therefore have a
significant effect on the reliability of the entire
system~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024,kalay_systematic_2025}.

Lifetime models attempt to quantify the relationship between thermal stress and
the number of thermal cycles a semiconductor device can withstand before
failure. One of the simplest is the Coffin--Manson
model~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025,albarbar_thermal_2018}:
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{f}} = \alpha \left(\Delta T_{\mathrm{j}}\right)^{\beta},
  \label{eq:coffin-manson}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the number of power cycles (in thousands) before
failure, $\Delta T_{\mathrm{j}}$ is the swing in junction temperature
$T_{\mathrm{j}}$, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are experimentally determined
parameters~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025}. This simplified form considers only the
temperature swing and omits the Arrhenius term for absolute temperature, which
becomes significant mainly above about \SI{200}{\celsius}~\cite{kalay_systematic_2025}.
\edited{Equation numbering in the draft started at (2.2) with no (2.1); equations
are now numbered automatically.}
The absolute temperature is nevertheless limited. The junction temperature of a
power semiconductor must be kept below its rated maximum, typically
\SIrange{125}{175}{\celsius} for silicon devices~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}, and
packaging limits the junction temperature of even silicon carbide (SiC) and
gallium nitride (GaN) devices to about \SI{175}{\celsius}~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024}.
\edited{The rated-maximum statement was cited to [Liu \emph{et al.}, wireless
charging]; Laloya \emph{et al.} is the source that states it (and the
\SIrange{125}{175}{\celsius} range), so the two citations were merged.
Grammar of the SiC/GaN clause fixed (``packaging limits even SiC \dots\ devices
limit'').}
Magnetic components are subject to similar
limits~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,mengesha_advanced_2025}; soft ferrites, for example,
have the lowest Curie temperature of the common soft magnetic
materials~\cite{li_review_2022}. Successful thermal management for high
reliability must therefore both hold the maximum temperature below these limits
and suppress the temperature swing $\Delta T_{\mathrm{j}}$.

Heat removal must also be achieved without degrading power density. In compact
systems, up to half of the volume can be required for the cooling
system~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}. Equally, the power consumed to move the coolant
is a burden on the converter: it must remain small relative to the power
processed if efficiency is to be maintained~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}. These failure
modes, volume and efficiency considerations translate directly into a set of
requirements for the cooling system, which are presented in
\cref{sec:trends} alongside the schemes available to meet them.

requirements are listed in \cref{sec:trends}.}

\subsection{Cooling in the Presence of Magnetic Components}
\label{sec:cooling-presence-magnetics}

Inductors and other magnetic components are required in most
PECs~\cite{morroni_understanding_nodate}, so a cooling method for
high-power-density converters must remain effective in their presence. Their
thermal design is also becoming harder as converter design
evolves~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. Higher switching frequencies at higher power
levels are increasingly accompanied by operating modes such as zero-voltage
switching (ZVS) or discontinuous conduction mode (DCM), which reduce switching
losses and lower the required inductance~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. These operating
modes result in relatively high inductor current ripple, which causes
substantial AC loss and temperature rise in the inductor~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.
Soft ferrite cores have a low saturation flux density, typically
\SIrange{0.2}{0.5}{\tesla}, so an air gap is often required, leading to fringing
magnetic flux~\cite{li_review_2022}. In a gapped inductor, the fringing flux can
increase the losses in the winding~\cite{ouyang_overview_2014}. Cooling systems
made of conductive materials and placed close to the windings also experience
increased losses~\cite{biela_cooling_2007,gao_modeling_2022}.

Gao \emph{et al.}\ identify temperature limits as a major constraint on the
design of such inductors and argue that improvements in thermal management are
needed to enable further size reduction~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. They also state
that effective thermal management solutions for planar magnetics optimised for
high-power, high-density applications, using cores such as ELP, are yet to be
developed~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}.

Because magnetic components cannot be removed from the converter, a cooling
method for high-power-density PECs must incur only minimal loss and must not
compromise their performance. This is particularly relevant to conductive
coolants such as liquid metal (\cref{sec:liquid-metal}); existing approaches to
cooling magnetic components are reviewed in \cref{sec:cooling-magnetics}.

\subsection{Trends in Electronics Cooling}
\label{sec:trends}

The failure mechanisms discussed in \cref{sec:thermal-management} translate into
four requirements for a converter cooling system. It must:
\begin{itemize}
  \item hold device temperatures below their maximum operating limit;
  \item suppress the temperature swing;
  \item be compact; and
  \item have a low burden (pumping) power.
\end{itemize}
Many schemes have been developed to handle increasingly high heat
fluxes~\cite{liu_thermal_2025,rahman_emerging_2024,laloya_heat_2016,mudawar_assessment_2001},
and they meet these requirements to varying degrees. It is useful to divide these
schemes broadly into single-phase and phase-change approaches, as they use
fundamentally different principles for heat removal.

\subsubsection*{Single-Phase Cooling}

Natural air convection is the simplest and cheapest scheme, but its low heat
removal capacity restricts it to devices dissipating only a few
watts~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024,laloya_heat_2016}. Forced air, in which fans drive
air across a heat sink, is used in many power converters and reaches heat fluxes
of tens of \si{\watt\per\centi\metre\squared}; its large volume and weight limit
the achievable power density~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}. The poor transport
properties of air ultimately confine it to low-heat-flux
devices~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}.

Single-phase liquid cooling, typically with water circulated through a cold
plate, pump and remote radiator, is therefore preferred at higher power
density~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024,laloya_heat_2016}. Cold plates can handle tens of
\si{\watt\per\centi\metre\squared}, and microchannel heat sinks approach
\SI{1000}{\watt\per\centi\metre\squared}~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}. Because the
coolant remains liquid, its flow rate provides an actuator with which to regulate
the device temperature. The weakness of water is its low thermal conductivity
$k$, about \SI{0.6}{\watt\per\metre\per\kelvin}~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. In
fully developed laminar flow the Nusselt number is constant, so the heat transfer
coefficient scales with $k/D_{\mathrm{h}}$ and can only be raised by shrinking
the hydraulic diameter $D_{\mathrm{h}}$~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Pressure drop
rises steeply as channels narrow, limiting further scaling and increasing pump
power~\cite{rahman_emerging_2024,laloya_heat_2016}. Studies of single-phase coolants
point to a practical minimum hydraulic diameter below which the likelihood of
clogging increases drastically~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}.
\edited{This citation was highlighted in the draft. Checked: Mudawar (2001)
does state that the likelihood of clogging ``increases very drastically'' at
small hydraulic diameters, so the citation is correct and the highlight was
removed.}

\subsubsection*{Phase-Change Cooling}

Phase change underpins many of the highest-heat-flux schemes. Absorbing latent
heat through boiling gives higher heat transfer rates and more isothermal
surfaces than single-phase cooling~\cite{laloya_heat_2016,mudawar_assessment_2001}.
These systems, however, are complex to
implement~\cite{laloya_heat_2016,mudawar_assessment_2001}. The central difficulty is
the critical heat flux (CHF). Beyond the CHF, vapour restricts liquid access to
the heated surface, causing the device temperature to rise uncontrollably; CHF is
therefore the upper design limit for any phase-change
system~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}. Because CHF depends on mass velocity,
subcooling, pressure, hydraulic diameter, heated length and surface
orientation~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}, it is difficult to predict, and the
safety margin required erodes the performance advantage of phase change.
Additionally, boiling tends to begin above the expected temperature, and the
subsequent sudden drop in temperature can thermally shock the
device~\cite{mudawar_assessment_2001}. Common implementations such as heat pipes
and phase-change materials are passive~\cite{laloya_heat_2016}, removing the
actuator through which the temperature swing could otherwise be regulated.

\subsubsection*{Summary and Implications for Liquid Metal}

A single-phase forced-liquid loop best meets the requirements for PEC thermal
management, because a controlled flow rate can be used to suppress temperature
swings; this is not possible with passive phase-change methods. With water,
however, performance is fundamentally limited by thermal conductivity, so higher
heat flux must be achieved with narrower channels. Narrow channels require
greater pumping power, directly increasing the burden on the power system. A
coolant with substantially higher thermal conductivity would raise the heat
transfer coefficient at a given channel size, and the search for such coolants
motivates research into liquid metals~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}, discussed in
\cref{sec:liquid-metal}. Many studies have considered this approach for
electronics, but the case of cooling magnetic components with liquid metal has
not yet been examined. Any cooling strategy that operates close to magnetic
components and their leakage fields can suffer increased eddy-current losses in
its metallic parts, as examined in \cref{sec:cooling-magnetics}.

\subsection{Cooling of Magnetic Components}
\label{sec:cooling-magnetics}

Magnetic components are conventionally cooled by convection from their exposed
surfaces, which is limited by the heat transfer coefficient of
air~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}. Planar constructions improve on this through their
high surface-area-to-volume ratio~\cite{gao_modeling_2022,ouyang_overview_2014}, but
a standard flat cold plate often still cools only one side of the core and falls
short of cooling the windings~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. Gao \emph{et al.}\
addressed this by engineering the thermal conduction path using a custom
aluminium cold plate, ceramic shim, aluminium bracket and thermal vias, which
achieved an improved power density~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. However, eddy currents
induced in the metal raised the measured AC winding resistance by
\SI{34}{\percent}~\cite{gao_modeling_2022}. Biela and Kolar similarly found that a
heat-transfer component near an inductor air gap experiences significant
eddy-current loss and must be recessed from it~\cite{biela_cooling_2007}. If a
conductive liquid metal is exposed to similar fields, the coolant itself will
incur losses. To the author's knowledge, this has not yet been assessed in the
literature.

\section{Liquid Metal as a Coolant}
\label{sec:liquid-metal}

Liquid metals have long been used as coolants where heat fluxes and operating
temperatures exceed those that conventional fluids can
tolerate~\cite{rowinski_small_2015}. Much of the early characterisation of liquid
metal cooling systems was carried out as fundamental research for nuclear
reactors, including small modular reactors (SMRs)~\cite{rowinski_small_2015},
which produce on the order of tens to hundreds of megawatts. These systems are
now at an advanced stage of research, with the switch to liquid metals driven
largely by their high-temperature stability. SMRs typically use lead or sodium
coolants, but lead is toxic and requires careful handling and
containment~\cite{oecd_handbook_2015}, whereas sodium is highly chemically
reactive, particularly with water and air, posing fire and explosion
hazards~\cite{foust_sodium-nak_1972}. These factors complicate broader commercial
deployment.

\subsection{Electromagnetic Pumping}
\label{sec:em-pumping}

The low volumetric heat capacity of Galinstan makes the pumping requirement more
demanding. For a given heat load $Q$ and coolant temperature rise $\Delta T$,
the required volumetric flow rate is
\begin{equation}
  \Vdot = \frac{Q}{\rho \, \cp \, \Delta T},
  \label{eq:flow-requirement}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the coolant density and $\cp$ its specific heat capacity. With
0.55 of the volumetric heat capacity of water~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
Galinstan needs about 1.8 times the flow rate of water for the same temperature
rise and heat load. The coolant temperature rise per unit heat load,
$\Delta T/Q = 1/(\rho\,\cp\,\Vdot)$, is known as the caloric (or heat-capacity)
resistance, which adds to the convective resistance at the heated surface. In
the optimised heat sinks reported by Hodes \emph{et al.}, caloric resistance
accounts for about \SI{55}{\percent} of the total thermal resistance for
Galinstan, compared with \SI{40}{\percent} for water~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
and An \emph{et al.}\ identify caloric resistance as the primary bottleneck for
liquid metal micro- and mini-channel heat sinks~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. The high
thermal conductivity partly offsets this, as it yields higher heat transfer
coefficients in wider channels~\cite{zhang_water-based_2015,an_liquid_2024}. However,
liquid metal requires more pumping power than water at the same volume flow
because of its higher viscosity~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Most comparisons between
water and Galinstan cooling are made at the chip scale, in mini- or
micro-channels. Pumps for electronics cooling are generally reported as
individual designs~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026,fan_liquid_2021,hart_magnetohydrodynamically_2025},
with only~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026} providing a comparison with an equivalent water
system.

Make the number and the citation agree.}

The electrical conductivity of Galinstan allows it to be driven by
electromagnetic (EM) pumps with no moving parts. These pumps are silent,
vibration-free and compact~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. The EM pump built in a
previous Part~IV project is a permanent-magnet DC conduction
pump~\cite{kwon_project_nodate,loh_project_nodate}, although pumps can also be based on
electromagnetic induction~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. In a conduction pump, a current
passed through the conductive fluid, perpendicular to a magnetic field, produces
a Lorentz force. The driving force, and therefore the flow rate, can be
increased by increasing the magnetic flux density or the driving
current~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Flow is usually adjusted by varying the driving
current~\cite{an_liquid_2024}, which allows the cooling to be modulated and
temperature swings to be suppressed. For electronics cooling, conduction pumps
typically operate at high current (\SIrange{1}{100}{\ampere}) and low voltage
(\SIrange{0.01}{0.5}{\volt}), consuming approximately
\SIrange{0.1}{50}{\watt}~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Their efficiency is low: up to
about \SI{10}{\percent} for a pump reported in~\cite{hodes_potential_2014} and
above \SI{6}{\percent} over most flow rates for a pump reviewed
in~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Even so, a conduction pump consumed less than
\SI{15}{\percent} of the power of a mechanical water pump at the same heating
power and cooling performance~\cite{an_liquid_2024}.
\edited{The current/voltage/power ranges were cited to Zhang \emph{et al.}
(2015); they come from An \emph{et al.} (2024), so the citation was corrected.
``only 15\,\%'' changed to ``less than 15\,\%'': An \emph{et al.} report
\SI{1.5}{\watt} against ``more than \SI{10}{\watt}''.}

Induction pumps instead induce current in the liquid metal through an
alternating or travelling magnetic field. They are more complex and larger, but
can produce greater driving forces~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Furthermore, the
magnetic field can be derived directly from the converter. Fan \emph{et al.}\
integrated a magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) pump into an inductor so that it pumps
the liquid metal while remaining part of the converter~\cite{fan_liquid_2021}.
Because the inductor is part of the converter being cooled, the flow rate
self-adjusts with converter loading~\cite{fan_liquid_2021}. As the field is
produced by the converter current, the coolant is exposed to alternating fields
and therefore to eddy-current losses~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026}.

In summary, permanent-magnet DC conduction pumps are usually preferred for
electronics cooling because of their simple construction and compact size.
Electromagnetic pumping, despite its low electrical-to-hydraulic efficiency,
provides effective heat transport at low pumping power.

\subsection{Compatibility with Electrical Equipment}
\label{sec:compatibility}

The use of Galinstan in commercial settings requires a high level of user safety
and compatibility with the electrical equipment. Three key concerns arise when
a new material is introduced into these environments: material safety, corrosion
and electromagnetic compatibility.

\subsubsection*{Material Safety}

Galinstan was developed as a non-toxic, non-flammable replacement for mercury in
thermometry~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Ga-based alloys are low-melting-point
metals that avoid the toxicity of mercury, the flammability of NaK
(sodium--potassium alloy) and the radioactivity of francium~\cite{an_liquid_2024}.
Galinstan's vapour pressure is negligible even at high temperatures, so it poses
no inhalation risk~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Galinstan does not pose a major
hazard, although long-term exposure to gallium oxide should be
avoided~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Gallium oxide can damage the respiratory
system at high airborne concentrations, but only minimal amounts are released
from a closed liquid metal loop~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. Prior prototypes managed
risks, including skin contact and spillage, using nitrile gloves, eye protection
and containment trays~\cite{kwon_project_nodate,loh_project_nodate}.

\subsubsection*{Corrosion and Chemical Compatibility}

Galinstan is widely described as corrosive to metals~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
although An \emph{et al.}\ report that Ga-based alloys are generally unreactive,
except that they corrode aluminium severely and copper surfaces require
protection above \SI{100}{\celsius}~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. This presents a
potential challenge for PEC hardware, as many conventional designs use aluminium
for structural components, heat sinks or electromagnetic shielding. Moreover, the
effect of Galinstan exposure on the mechanical properties of ductile metals
(e.g.\ aluminium) remains to be evaluated~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. The high
velocities required to reduce the caloric resistance also raise concern about
erosion, which has yet to be studied~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Plastics or
nickel-plated metals address both corrosion and surface
oxide~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}, and ceramic substrates such as aluminium nitride
are compatible with Ga-based alloys~\cite{an_liquid_2024} while being both
thermally conductive and electrically insulating.
\edited{Sentence fragment (``Although oxidation is severe on aluminium \dots'')
merged into the previous sentence. An \emph{et al.} describe the effect on
aluminium as \emph{corrosion}, not oxidation, so the wording follows the
source.}

\subsubsection*{Electrical Hazards and Electromagnetic Compatibility}

With a conductivity of about \SI{3.3e6}{\siemens\per\metre}~\cite{hodes_potential_2014},
Galinstan is a conductor wherever it flows. Its high surface tension resists
seepage through small cracks at moderate pressure, but a leak onto energised
circuitry is a direct short, and the electronics must be de-energised before
clean-up~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. The loop also galvanically connects every
wetted metal part, so a cooling system design must consider how the EM pump is
energised, as the pump supply can also energise any metallic radiator and cold
plate~\cite{kwon_project_nodate}.

AC fields from nearby magnetics induce eddy currents in the conductive
coolant~\cite{an_liquid_2024}. The skin depth in Galinstan,
$\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f \mu_0 \sigma}$, calculated from the conductivity above,
is about \SI{0.95}{\milli\metre} at \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz} and about
\SI{0.55}{\milli\metre} at \SI{250}{\kilo\hertz}. Given Galinstan's
incompatibility with many metals, plastic piping may be preferred for low cost;
near magnetics, where alternating magnetic fields are present, nickel-plated
metals may be a better choice to manage eddy-current heating. Channel placement
and geometry may therefore govern the choice of piping material.
\edited{Skin-depth expression added so the quoted values can be checked
(they are correct for $\sigma = \SI{3.3e6}{\siemens\per\metre}$).}
\issue{Nickel is ferromagnetic, so a nickel-plated pipe in an \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz}
field will itself see eddy-current and hysteresis loss. Your own simulation

\section{Research Gap and Objectives}
\label{sec:research-gap}

\subsection{Research Gap}

Single-phase water cooling is bounded by the thermal conductivity of
water~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. Galinstan has a much higher thermal
conductivity, which can improve heat transfer~\cite{hodes_potential_2014}. This
study aims to address two gaps identified in the literature.

The first is the lack of comparisons between water and liquid metal at the scale
of a converter's cooling loop. Existing comparisons are made for micro- and
mini-channel heat
sinks~\cite{hodes_potential_2014,zhang_water-based_2015,zhang_experimental_2019,an_liquid_2024},
or lie at the scale of nuclear reactors, which use different metals at much
higher temperatures~\cite{rowinski_small_2015}. This study aims to quantify the
trade-offs of the Galinstan system (as designed
in~\cite{kwon_project_nodate,loh_project_nodate}) relative to a comparable water-cooling
system.

The second is the cooling of magnetic components with Galinstan. To the author's
knowledge, no study has demonstrated Galinstan cooling of a magnetic component,
such as a power-dense inductor, or quantified the eddy-current losses induced in
the coolant by its leakage field. Eddy-current losses have been assessed in AC
conduction pumps~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026}, and an inductor has been used to drive a
pump~\cite{fan_ultra-efficient_2026,fan_liquid_2021}, but not to cool the inductor itself
directly. This matters because most converters contain inductors, transformers
or other magnetics. In compact systems, even a cooling system that does not
directly cool the magnetics is close to them and must therefore tolerate their
leakage fields.

This project addresses both gaps using the existing Galinstan loop of
Loh~\cite{loh_project_nodate} and Kwon~\cite{kwon_project_nodate}. The loop comprises a
permanent-magnet MHD pump, perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) tubing, a cold plate and a
fan-cooled radiator, and can remove up to a \SI{72}{\watt} resistive
load~\cite{kwon_project_nodate,loh_project_nodate}. The loop was not tested against water
in the same apparatus, and its experiments did not consider magnetics:
Loh~\cite{loh_project_nodate} focused on switching devices because Galinstan was
unlikely to interfere with them, unlike magnetics, and Kwon~\cite{kwon_project_nodate}
excluded direct integration and testing with magnetics because of time
limitations and uncertainty about electromagnetic interference. Both recommended
comparison with water cooling, and Kwon also recommended testing higher heat
loads and investigating the interaction with inductive pad
fields~\cite{kwon_project_nodate,loh_project_nodate}.
\edited{``This project was not tested against water'' referred to the previous
project's loop, not this project; reworded to ``The loop was not tested
\dots''.}
