\textbf{\large ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

% Write your abstract here
Inductive power transfer (IPT) chargers for electric vehicles must remove
hundreds of watts of loss from compact power electronics, resonant circuits and
large magnetic pads. As the industry
pushes for more power-dense converters, thermal management increasingly limits
how small they can become. Galinstan, a liquid metal at room temperature with
about 28 times the thermal conductivity of water, offers a potential solution. It
can be circulated by a silent, low-power magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) pump with no
moving parts. However, its low volumetric heat capacity and high electrical
conductivity bring trade-offs, and few studies have examined where in a converter
it would be most effective.

This project assessed the feasibility of Galinstan cooling for IPT systems. A
Galinstan loop and a water loop were tested on the same commercial cold plate
under resistive heat loads. The results were interpreted with an
$\varepsilon$--NTU thermal network and extended to higher flows with a conjugate
heat transfer (CHT) simulation. The Galinstan loop was also used to cool a
power-dense \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz} inductor, and the eddy-current loss induced in
the coolant was measured.

At the flows each pump could deliver, water outperformed Galinstan. The total
thermal resistance was \SIrange{0.257}{0.267}{\celsius\per\watt} with water
against \SIrange{0.488}{0.799}{\celsius\per\watt} with Galinstan, a difference
well beyond the measurement uncertainty. At
\SIrange{0.77}{1.45}{\milli\litre\per\second}, Galinstan's high conductivity made
convection negligible, but its low heat capacity limited how much heat the flow
could carry away. The MHD pump drew only \SI{0.85}{\watt} and achieved a higher
ratio of heat removed to pumping power than the water pump, a coefficient of
performance of 200 against 47.6, although its mechanical efficiency was just
\SI{0.56}{\percent}. The CHT simulation shows
that at \SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}, the limit of laminar flow, the Galinstan
total resistance would fall to \SI{0.29}{\celsius\per\watt}, within
\SI{14}{\percent} of water. The $\varepsilon$--NTU model also indicates that
Galinstan outperforms water once the heated area falls below about
\SI{40}{\centi\metre\squared}. In the inductor test, the Galinstan loop cooled an
inductor dissipating more than twice the loss its core can handle without active
cooling, and the Galinstan inside the metal cold plate contributed only
minimally to the inductor's loss.

Galinstan is therefore best suited to small, high-flux components of an IPT
system, such as the inverter and rectifier modules and compact magnetics, rather
than the large, distributed losses of the pads. Realising this advantage will
require a more efficient, higher-flow pump and a smaller cold plate designed for
liquid metal.
