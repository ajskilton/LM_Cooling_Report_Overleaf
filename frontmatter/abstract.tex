\textbf{\large ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

% Write your abstract here
Inductive power transfer (IPT) chargers for electric vehicles must remove
hundreds of watts of loss from compact power electronics and from magnetics that
operate in strong \SI{85}{\kilo\hertz} fields. The industry is driving for more power-dense converters, but thermal management
increasingly limits how far their power density can rise. Galinstan, a
room-temperature liquid metal with about 28 times the thermal conductivity of
water, offers a potential solution. Galinstan can be driven by a low-power 
magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) pump that is completely silent and has no moving parts.
However, its low volumetric heat capacity and high electrical conductivity bring
trade-offs that few studies analysed to provide advice on where the coolant might be most effectively utilised. 
This project assessed the feasibility of Galinstan cooling for IPT systems. A
Galinstan loop and a water loop were compared on the same commercial cold plate
under resistive heat loads; the results were interpreted with an
$\varepsilon$--NTU thermal network and extended with a conjugate heat transfer
(CHT) simulation, and the Galinstan loop was used to cool a power-dense
\SI{85}{\kilo\hertz} inductor while the eddy-current loss in the coolant was
measured.

At the flows each pump could deliver, water outperformed Galinstan, with a total
thermal resistance of \SIrange{0.257}{0.267}{\celsius\per\watt} against
\SIrange{0.488}{0.799}{\celsius\per\watt}, a difference well beyond the
measurement uncertainty. At \SIrange{0.77}{1.45}{\milli\litre\per\second}, the
Galinstan cold plate was caloric-limited: its high conductivity made the
convective resistance negligible, and its low heat capacity set the
performance. The MHD pump drew only \SI{0.85}{\watt}, but with a mechanical
efficiency of \SI{0.56}{\percent}. The CHT simulation shows that at
\SI{5}{\milli\litre\per\second}, the limit of laminar flow, the Galinstan total
resistance would fall to \SI{0.29}{\celsius\per\watt}, within \SI{14}{\percent}
of water, and the $\varepsilon$--NTU model predicts that Galinstan outperforms
water once the heated area falls below about \SI{40}{\centi\metre\squared}. The
Galinstan loop cooled the inductor at more than twice the loss its core can
dissipate without active cooling, while the Galinstan inside the cold plate added
only \SI{1.0}{\milli\ohm} to the winding AC resistance, within the repeatability
of the measurement, because the metal of the plate shields it from the leakage
field. Galinstan is therefore best suited to small, high-flux components of an
IPT system, such as the inverter and rectifier modules and compact magnetics,
rather than the large, distributed losses of the pads. Realising this advantage
will require a more efficient, higher-flow pump and a smaller cold plate designed
for liquid metal.
